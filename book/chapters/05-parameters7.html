5  The parameters7 package

Chapter 2 describes how a constrained scalar is carried onto the real line by a link function. A modeling toolkit meets constrained parameters that are not scalars as well: the covariance of a multivariate response, the precision of a random effect, the penalty matrix of a smooth term, a probability vector on the simplex, the transition matrix of a Markov chain. This chapter presents the objects parameters7 uses for them – a parametrization of the set where the parameter lives, with exact derivatives to fourth order on the unconstrained scale – and explains why they are a separate construction rather than a vector- or matrix-valued link.

The mathematics of differentiating a function of a matrix with respect to a parametrization of that matrix is classical, and Magnus and Neudecker (1999) is the standard reference. The unconstrained parametrizations themselves come from Pinheiro and Bates (1996), whose paper is the origin of the log-Cholesky map used here, and Pourahmadi (2011) reviews the field.

Section 5.1 sets out what a constrained parameter is: a map from an unconstrained vector to a value in a constrained set, the derivatives of that map, and, when the value is a symmetric matrix, the quantities a likelihood asks of it. It explains why the Jacobian of such a map is not diagonal and why that single fact rules out treating the construction as a link.

Section 5.2 presents the families the package ships. The log-Cholesky map is the general positive definite case, and its log-determinant turns out to be linear in the free vector; the matrix logarithm is the other chart on the same set, with the log-determinant linear and the inverse exact by construction. The diagonal families are where this package composes with linkfunctions7 rather than competing with it, and the scaled fixed matrix is the case a penalty needs. The simplex and the transition matrix leave the matrix branch altogether: their values are a probability vector and a row-stochastic matrix, and their derivatives close over the value itself.

Section 5.3 treats rank-deficient matrices. Such a matrix is not a defect here but the definition of a penalty, and the section explains what has to change to accommodate one: the determinant becomes a pseudo-determinant, the inverse becomes a solve restricted to the range, and the rank itself has to be determined in a way that does not depend on the arithmetic that produced the matrix. Section 5.4 closes the chapter with the census of the families and the contract exercised call by call.

5.1 Constrained parameters

5.1.1 The map and its free vector

Let \(\mathcal{M}\) be a set of symmetric \(p \times p\) matrices defined by a constraint: positive definite matrices, positive definite matrices with a prescribed pattern of zeros, correlation matrices, or positive semidefinite matrices of a fixed null space. A parameter is a map

\[ M : \mathbb{R}^{d} \longrightarrow \mathcal{M}, \qquad \eta \longmapsto M(\eta), \tag{5.1}\]

whose image is the whole of \(\mathcal{M}\) and which is one-to-one, together with the derivatives of that map and the quantities a likelihood asks of its value. The vector \(\eta\) is called free because it carries no constraint: every point of \(\mathbb{R}^{d}\) is admissible, and an optimizer may take any step from any point without leaving the domain. This is the arrangement a link function provides for a scalar, and it exists for the same reason: the constraint is absorbed into the map so that the estimation problem is unconstrained.

The number \(d\) of free values is the dimension of \(\mathcal{M}\) as a manifold, not \(p^2\) or \(p(p+1)/2\) in general. A full positive definite matrix has \(d = p(p+1)/2\); a diagonal one has \(d = p\); a matrix that is a fixed matrix times an unknown scale has \(d = 1\). A parameter therefore owns its dimension: a map for \(3 \times 3\) matrices and a map for \(4 \times 4\) matrices are different objects, with different \(d\) and different names for the free values, and both are needed before any data exist because they determine the shape of the parameter vector.

Each free value carries a label, and the labels are fixed at construction. The distribution of Section 3.5 builds its parameter names from them, the derivative components of the likelihood are keyed by them, and the printed summary of a fit reports them, so they are part of the interface rather than a convenience.

A label names the coordinate and not the quantity the coordinate produces, which is a distinction the families here keep in their spelling. Where a link carries a constrained quantity onto the free scale, the label records that link: a variance appears as log_scale and a correlation as z_rho, the tag naming the transformation rather than the bound it removes. Where the coordinate is already unrestricted the label is the plain name of the quantity, as the below-diagonal entries L2.1 of a Cholesky factor are. The consequence of getting this wrong is visible outside the parameter: a consumer flattens the free vector into scalar parameters carrying identity links, so a label promising a correlation would report a number on a scale where the interval around it can reach past one.

5.1.2 Why this is not a link

A link function of Chapter 2 is a scalar map \(g\) applied to each parameter separately. Applied to a vector of parameters, it produces a diagonal Jacobian, which is what makes the chain rule of Section 3.1.4 factorize: the \(k\)th derivative in the \(k\)th direction involves only \(h^{(j)}\) for that one coordinate, and Faà di Bruno’s formula reduces to a product of partial Bell polynomials in one variable.

A matrix parametrization has no such parameter. Changing one free value of a log-Cholesky map changes an entire row and column of the matrix it produces, so \(\partial M / \partial \eta_k\) is a matrix rather than a number and the Jacobian \(\partial \mathrm{vech}(M) / \partial \eta\) is dense. The chain rule still holds, but it is a matrix chain rule, and the quantities a consumer needs from it are traces rather than products. Nothing that linkfunctions7 computes would be reusable, and a consumer that treated a matrix parametrization as a link would produce plausible but wrong numbers.

The two constructions therefore stay apart, and meet at the one place where they agree, which is a diagonal matrix. There the Jacobian is diagonal, and parameters7 reuses the link objects exactly as they are rather than writing its own positive maps; see Section 5.2.2.

5.1.3 What a consumer asks

A likelihood that carries a matrix parameter asks four things of it, and a parameter answers all four. A fifth is asked not by the likelihood but by whoever reads its result, and is described at the end of this section.

The matrix itself, \(M(\eta)\), is the only compulsory one; everything else has a numerical method available from it alone.

The derivatives \(A_k = \partial M/\partial \eta_k\) and \(A_{kl} = \partial^2 M/\partial \eta_k \partial \eta_l\) are what the score and the observed information of Section 3.5.2 are built from. Only the \(d(d+1)/2\) distinct second derivatives are computed, keyed by the same enumeration that names them. Recovering an index by splitting a name on its separator fails for a label that itself contains the separator, so the package generates indices and names from one enumeration and the two cannot disagree.

The log-determinant \(\log|M|\) and its first two derivatives are the normalizing constant of a gaussian and its derivatives. The identity behind them is

\[ \frac{\partial \log|M|}{\partial \eta_k} = \mathrm{tr}\!\left(M^{-1} A_k\right), \tag{5.2}\]

which is Magnus and Neudecker (1999)’s, and which makes a closed-form log-determinant derivative something other than an independent claim: it must agree with the derivative of the matrix through Equation 5.2, and the package’s validator compares the two routes.

The solve \(M^{-1}B\) is what a quadratic form needs. It is computed through a factorization rather than by forming an inverse, which is both cheaper and better conditioned; a family with a closed-form factor supplies it, and the rest obtain one from the matrix.

s <- parameters7::log_cholesky(3)
s
#> Parameter: log_cholesky
#> Matrix:    3 x 3, symmetric
#> Rank:      3 of 3
#> Role:      either
#> 
#> Free values: 6
#>   log_L1, log_L2, log_L3, L2.1, L3.1, L3.2
#> 
#> From the base class: none

eta <- c(0.1, -0.2, 0.3, 0.5, -0.4, 0.2)
round(parameters7::param_value(s, eta), 4)
#>         v1      v2      v3
#> v1  1.2214  0.5526 -0.4421
#> v2  0.5526  0.9203 -0.0363
#> v3 -0.4421 -0.0363  2.0221

The fifth question is what the family is about, and it is asked after the fitting rather than during it. The free values are coordinates, and for the economical families of Section 5.2.5 the quantity behind a coordinate is a variance or a correlation that no part of the estimated matrix displays on its own; for an autoregression the coefficients are not in the matrix at all. A parameter therefore declares those quantities together with the Jacobian of the map from the free vector and the scale each of them should be intervalled on, and a consumer carries its variance matrix onto them by the delta method, as Section 3.5.7 describes for a fitted model. The declaration is optional: a family whose matrix is the whole of what it has to say declares nothing, and a consumer already reports the matrix.

The sign in front of the log-determinant is the consumer’s arithmetic and not the parameter’s. A gaussian written in the covariance carries \(-\frac{1}{2}\log|\Sigma|\) and one written in the precision carries \(+\frac{1}{2}\log|\Omega|\), so the parameter returns \(\log|M|\) for the matrix it parametrizes and lets the distribution attach the sign. The same holds for the two derivatives.

5.1.4 The inverse map

A parameter also answers the reverse question: given a matrix in \(\mathcal{M}\), which free vector produces it? This is what allows a fit to be started from a sample covariance, and what allows a marginal of Section 3.5.5 to be expressed in the free values of a smaller parameter.

The inverse map is implemented exactly or not at all, never by optimization. An optimization-based inverse would return a plausible \(\eta\) for a matrix outside \(\mathcal{M}\), indistinguishable from a correct answer. A family whose map has no closed-form inverse therefore signals an error, and so does the base class on behalf of any parameter that does not implement one.

m <- parameters7::param_value(s, eta)
max(abs(parameters7::param_free(s, m) - eta))
#> [1] 6.938894e-17

5.1.5 What a new parameter has to write

Only Equation 5.1 is compulsory. A parameter that implements nothing but the matrix receives numerical methods for the derivatives, the log-determinant and its derivatives, and the solve, registered on the base class exactly as the numerical fallbacks of Section 3.4 are registered for a distribution. It is therefore usable the moment it exists, and each closed form supplied later replaces the corresponding numerical method through dispatch, with no change to anything that consumes it.

The numerical derivatives obey the rule of Section 3.4: one central difference applied to the highest analytical order available, never a chain of first differences. A parameter that supplies its own first derivative gets a second derivative accurate to the square root of machine precision rather than to its fourth root.

The validator check_parameter() puts a parameter through the same checks the shipped families face. It compares the derivatives against finite differences, the log-determinant against the determinant of the assembled matrix, its derivative against Equation 5.2, and the solve against a direct solution, and it reports which orders came from a closed form and which from the fallback, for the reason check_link() does: an order that comes from a fallback would otherwise be compared against a numerical differentiation of the order below, which is the same arithmetic twice and agrees however wrong the parameter is.

parameters7::check_parameter(s, verbose = TRUE)
#> Parameter: log_cholesky   (3 x 3, rank 3, 6 free)
#>   [OK         ] membership           0.00e+00
#>   [OK         ] round trip           7.77e-16
#>   [OK         ] first derivatives    2.90e-11
#>   [OK         ] second derivatives   3.67e-11
#>   [OK         ] log-determinant      2.89e-15
#>   [OK         ] logdet gradient      7.48e-14
#>   [OK         ] logdet hessian       0.00e+00

5.2 The families

5.2.1 The log-Cholesky map

The general positive definite case is parametrized through the Cholesky factor. Every positive definite \(M\) has a unique factorization \(M = L L^\top\) with \(L\) lower triangular and \(L_{ii} > 0\), and the correspondence is a bijection. Taking the logarithm of the diagonal removes the only remaining constraint, so

\[ \eta = \bigl(\log L_{11}, \ldots, \log L_{pp},\; L_{21}, L_{31}, \ldots, L_{p,p-1}\bigr) \tag{5.3}\]

ranges over the whole of \(\mathbb{R}^{d}\) with \(d = p(p+1)/2\), and \(M(\eta) = L L^\top\) is onto the positive definite cone. This is the parametrization of Pinheiro and Bates (1996). The ordering above is fixed rather than incidental: the diagonal comes first, then the below-diagonal entries column by column, and the labels of the free values follow it.

The logarithm on the diagonal is intrinsic to the map and not a swappable link. A different positive map would give a different parametrization of the same set, but it would not compose with anything, since the constraint being removed is on the factor and not on an entry of \(M\).

Differentiating is straightforward once the factor is written as a function of the free vector. Let \(L_k = \partial L/\partial \eta_k\), which is the matrix with a single non-zero entry: \(L_{ii}\) itself in a diagonal direction, because the entry is \(e^{\eta}\), and \(1\) in a below-diagonal direction. Then

\[ A_k = L_k L^\top + L L_k^\top , \qquad A_{kl} = L_k L_l^\top + L_l L_k^\top + \delta_{kl}^{\,\mathrm{diag}} \left( L_k L^\top + L L_k^\top \right), \tag{5.4}\]

where the last term appears only when \(k = l\) and the direction is a diagonal one, since \(\partial^2 L/\partial \eta_k^2 = L_k\) there and vanishes elsewhere. The factor is linear in every below-diagonal free value, which is why nothing else survives the second derivative.

The log-determinant is the principal convenience of this parametrization. Since \(|M| = |L|^2\) and \(L\) is triangular,

\[ \log|M| = 2\sum_{i=1}^{p} \log L_{ii} = 2\sum_{i \in \mathrm{diag}} \eta_i , \tag{5.5}\]

which is linear in the free vector. Its gradient is the constant \(2\) in each diagonal direction and \(0\) in every other, and its Hessian vanishes identically. A term that would otherwise cost a trace per component per observation costs nothing at all.

s <- parameters7::log_cholesky(3)
parameters7::param_dlogdet(s, c(0.1, -0.2, 0.3, 0.5, -0.4, 0.2))
#> log_L1 log_L2 log_L3   L2.1   L3.1   L3.2 
#>      2      2      2      0      0      0
parameters7::param_dlogdet(s, c(-2, 3, 0.7, -1.1, 0.4, 2.5))
#> log_L1 log_L2 log_L3   L2.1   L3.1   L3.2 
#>      2      2      2      0      0      0

5.2.2 Diagonal matrices

A diagonal matrix with positive entries is the case in which a matrix parametrization and a scalar link agree. The Jacobian of

\[ M(\eta) = \mathrm{diag}\bigl(h(\eta_1), \ldots, h(\eta_p)\bigr) \tag{5.6}\]

is diagonal, which is exactly the contract a link satisfies, so parameters7 takes \(h\) from linkfunctions7 instead of writing a positive map of its own. Any link whose range is the positive half-line serves, and its exact derivatives to fourth order come with it. The derivatives of the parameter are then \(A_k = h'(\eta_k) e_k e_k^\top\) and \(A_{kk} = h''(\eta_k) e_k e_k^\top\), with every mixed second derivative zero, and the log-determinant is \(\sum_i \log h(\eta_i)\).

parameters7::param_value(parameters7::diagonal_matrix(3), c(0, 0.5, -0.5))
#>    v1       v2        v3
#> v1  1 0.000000 0.0000000
#> v2  0 1.648721 0.0000000
#> v3  0 0.000000 0.6065307
parameters7::param_value(
  parameters7::diagonal_matrix(2, link = linkfunctions7::sqrt_link()), c(1, 2)
)
#>    v1 v2
#> v1  1  0
#> v2  0  4

A single free value shared by every entry gives \(\sigma^2 I\), which is the independent case a variance component or a ridge starts from. It is the same construction with \(d = 1\).

5.2.3 A fixed matrix carried by one scale

The third family is different in kind. Here the matrix is

\[ M(\eta) = \lambda P, \qquad \lambda = h(\eta), \tag{5.7}\]

with \(P\) a known symmetric positive semidefinite matrix and \(\lambda\) the only unknown. This is the commonest penalty in semiparametric regression: \(P\) may be the Gram matrix of a basis derivative from Section 6.3, a difference penalty \(\Delta^\top \Delta\), a neighborhood matrix, or the identity, in which case the parameter is a ridge.

Nothing here needs deriving. With the default log link, \(\lambda = e^{\eta}\) and every quantity is a constant times a function of the scale:

\[ \partial_\eta M = M, \qquad \partial^2_\eta M = M, \]

\[ \log|M|_{+} = r\,\eta + \log|P|_{+}, \qquad \partial_\eta \log|M|_{+} = r, \qquad \partial^2_\eta \log|M|_{+} = 0, \tag{5.8}\]

with \(r\) the rank of \(P\) and \(|\cdot|_{+}\) the pseudo-determinant of Section 5.3.2. The constant \(\log|P|_{+}\) is computed once at construction, since \(P\) does not change.

# a ridge: the identity, scaled
r <- parameters7::scaled_matrix(diag(4))
c(rank = r@rank, logdet_at_zero = parameters7::param_logdet(r, 0))
#>           rank logdet_at_zero 
#>              4              0

# a second-difference penalty on six coefficients
dm <- diff(diag(6), differences = 2)
pen <- parameters7::scaled_matrix(crossprod(dm))
c(dimension = pen@dimension, rank = pen@rank)
#> dimension      rank 
#>         6         4

5.2.4 Correlation matrices

A correlation matrix is a covariance whose diagonal is fixed at one, and that fixing is what makes it awkward: the constraint is not a bound on each entry but a relation among them, so a parametrization that produced the entries directly would have to repair the diagonal afterwards. The spherical parametrization avoids the repair by building the Cholesky factor rather than the matrix. Row \(i\) of \(L\) is a point on the unit sphere of \(\mathbb{R}^{i}\) written in angular coordinates, \[L_{ij} = \cos\theta_{ij} \prod_{k<j} \sin\theta_{ik} \quad (j < i), \qquad L_{ii} = \prod_{k<i} \sin\theta_{ik},\] with \(\theta_{ik} \in (0, \pi)\). The squares of the entries of a row telescope to one, so \(R = LL^{\top}\) has a unit diagonal whatever the angles are, and it is positive definite because \(L\) is triangular with a positive diagonal. The construction is that of Rapisarda, Brigo, and Mercurio (2007), and the angles reach the real line through the bounded link of Chapter 2, so the free values are unconstrained and no combination of them leaves the set.

The derivatives are the Leibniz rule of Section 5.2.1 applied to a different factor. Each entry of \(L\) is a product of sines and cosines of angles that each depend on one free value, so differentiating it replaces one factor by its derivative and leaves the rest alone. The rows of \(L\) are independent, which makes a derivative of the factor across two rows vanish; the same is not true of \(R\), whose entry \((i, j)\) is the inner product of rows \(i\) and \(j\), and a second derivative across those rows is supported on exactly those two entries.

The log-determinant is a second convenience of this chart. The factor is triangular, so \(\lvert R \rvert = \prod_i L_{ii}^{2}\) and \[\log\lvert R \rvert = 2 \sum_{i,k} \log \sin \theta_{ik},\] a sum with one term per free value. It is therefore separable: every mixed derivative is exactly zero and the pure ones are a logarithm composed with a sine.

s <- correlation_matrix(4)
eta <- c(0.4, -0.2, 0.6, 0.1, -0.5, 0.3)
r <- param_value(s, eta)
round(r, 4)
#>         v1      v2      v3      v4
#> v1  1.0000 -0.3051  0.1559 -0.0784
#> v2 -0.3051  1.0000 -0.4632  0.3802
#> v3  0.1559 -0.4632  1.0000 -0.3653
#> v4 -0.0784  0.3802 -0.3653  1.0000
diag(r)
#> v1 v2 v3 v4 
#>  1  1  1  1

# the log-determinant needs no factorization
c(closed = param_logdet(s, eta),
  spectral = sum(log(eigen(r, symmetric = TRUE, only.values = TRUE)$values)))
#>    closed  spectral 
#> -0.552623 -0.552623

5.2.5 Compound symmetry and AR(1)

An unstructured covariance over \(p\) occasions has \(p(p+1)/2\) free values, which at \(p = 20\) is 210 and beyond what a handful of subjects identifies. The two classical structured families collapse that count to two whatever \(p\) is, which is the reason they are used at all.

Compound symmetry is the exchangeable covariance \[M = \sigma^{2}\{(1-\rho)I + \rho J\},\] every variance equal and every correlation equal. Its eigenvalues are \(\sigma^{2}\{1 + (p-1)\rho\}\) once and \(\sigma^{2}(1-\rho)\) with multiplicity \(p-1\), so it is positive definite exactly when \[-\frac{1}{p-1} < \rho < 1,\] a bound that depends on the dimension and is the reason the correlation is carried by a link onto that interval rather than onto \((-1, 1)\): a chart that allowed \(\rho = -0.9\) at \(p = 5\) would hand a consumer an indefinite matrix at an ordinary free value. The eigenvalues also give the log-determinant, \[\log\lvert M \rvert = p \log \sigma^{2} + \log\{1 + (p-1)\rho\} + (p-1)\log(1-\rho),\] a sum of a function of one free value and a function of the other, so its mixed derivatives vanish identically. The inverse is compound symmetric again, by the Sherman-Morrison identity, so the family is closed under the choice of side: an exchangeable covariance has an exchangeable precision.

The AR(1) family is the covariance of a first-order autoregression, \(M_{ij} = \sigma^{2}\rho^{\lvert i-j \rvert}\), and it is definite for every \(\lvert \rho \rvert < 1\), so its correlation needs no dimension-dependent bound. Its determinant is \(\sigma^{2p}(1-\rho^{2})^{p-1}\) and its inverse is tridiagonal, which is the property the family is used for: an autoregression of order one is a Markov process, and a Markov process has no partial correlation beyond the lag, so the precision has no entries beyond the first off-diagonal.

That inverse is also what makes an AR(1) covariance and an AR(1) precision different models. The inverse of a compound-symmetric matrix is compound symmetric, so imposing the structure on either side gives the same family; the inverse of an AR(1) is tridiagonal but not AR(1), so the two impositions are genuinely different assumptions about the same data. This is what the role label of Section 5.2.10 records.

The two families differ in one respect that matters for the derivatives. The compound-symmetric pattern \(I + \rho(J - I)\) is linear in the correlation, so every derivative in it is the matching derivative of the correlation times a constant matrix. The AR(1) pattern is not: an entry is \(\rho^{m}\) for the lag \(m\), and its derivatives are a power composed with the link, which is the chain rule of Faà di Bruno taken to fourth order.

c(unstructured = log_cholesky(20)@n_free,
  compound_symmetry = compound_symmetry(20)@n_free,
  ar1 = ar1(20)@n_free)
#>      unstructured compound_symmetry               ar1 
#>               210                 2                 2

a <- ar1(5)
eta <- c(log(2), atanh(0.6))
round(param_solve(a, eta), 4)
#>         [,1]    [,2]    [,3]    [,4]    [,5]
#> [1,]  0.7812 -0.4688  0.0000  0.0000  0.0000
#> [2,] -0.4688  1.0625 -0.4688  0.0000  0.0000
#> [3,]  0.0000 -0.4688  1.0625 -0.4688  0.0000
#> [4,]  0.0000  0.0000 -0.4688  1.0625 -0.4688
#> [5,]  0.0000  0.0000  0.0000 -0.4688  0.7812

5.2.6 Autoregressions of any order

The AR(1) family generalizes, and the generalization is instructive because the obvious parametrization fails. A stationary autoregression of order \(q\) has coefficients \(\phi\) whose polynomial \(1 - \phi_1 z - \cdots - \phi_q z^q\) has all its roots outside the unit circle, and that region is not a box: already at \(q = 2\) it is the triangle \(\lvert\phi_2\rvert < 1\), \(\phi_2 \pm \phi_1 < 1\). No collection of scalar links onto intervals covers a set whose constraints tie the coordinates together, so the chart cannot be built one coefficient at a time.

The partial autocorrelations can. Each \(r_k\) lies in \((-1,1)\) independently of the others, and the Levinson-Durbin recursion carries them onto the stationary coefficients bijectively, which is the transformation of Barndorff-Nielsen and Schou (1973). Writing \(\phi^{(k)}\) for the coefficients of the best linear predictor of order \(k\), \[\phi^{(k)}_k = r_k, \qquad \phi^{(k)}_j = \phi^{(k-1)}_j - r_k \phi^{(k-1)}_{k-j},\] and the Yule-Walker equations at order \(k\) give the autocorrelations \[\rho_k = \sum_{j<k} \phi^{(k)}_j \rho_{k-j} + r_k,\] after which \(\rho_h = \sum_j \phi_j \rho_{h-j}\) at every lag beyond the order. Every free vector is then stationary by construction, and each partial autocorrelation takes the same link the AR(1) correlation takes.

The recursion is built from sums and products alone, so the map from the free values to the matrix is a polynomial one. Its derivatives are obtained by propagating the derivative arrays through it in compiled code – each intermediate quantity held together with all its partial derivatives to fourth order, combined by the product rule written out per order, the same Leibniz rule of Section 5.2.1 in its scalar form – rather than by expanding the recursion, which would be pages of algebra checkable only against the finite differences this construction exists to avoid.

Two quantities stay closed. The innovation variances of the recursion fall by \(1 - r_k^2\) at each of the first \(q\) steps and are constant after, so \[\log\lvert M \rvert = p\log\gamma_0 + \sum_{k=1}^{q}(p-k)\log(1-r_k^2),\] one term per free value and therefore separable. And the precision is banded of width \(q\): an autoregression of order \(q\) is Markov of order \(q\), so no partial correlation survives beyond the lag, and the inverse is assembled from the prediction form rather than factorized.

s <- autoregressive(7, order = 2)
eta <- c(log(2), atanh(0.7), atanh(-0.3))

# the implied coefficients are stationary whatever the free values are
round(param_solve(s, eta), 4)
#>         [,1]    [,2]    [,3]    [,4]    [,5]    [,6]    [,7]
#> [1,]  1.0774 -0.9804  0.3232  0.0000  0.0000  0.0000  0.0000

5.2.7 The simplex and the transition matrix

A probability vector \(\pi\) on the open simplex satisfies \(\pi_a > 0\) and \(\sum_a \pi_a = 1\), so a vector of \(K\) categories has \(K - 1\) free values. The package carries it by the additive log-ratio map with the last category as reference, \[\pi_a = \frac{e^{\eta_a}}{1 + \sum_b e^{\eta_b}}, \qquad \pi_K = \frac{1}{1 + \sum_b e^{\eta_b}},\] whose inverse is \(\eta_a = \log(\pi_a / \pi_K)\), exact. The derivatives of this map close over the value itself: writing \(Z = 1 + \sum_b e^{\eta_b}\), each \(\pi_a\) with \(a < K\) is a first derivative of \(\log Z\), so the derivative arrays of \(\pi\) are the higher derivatives of \(\log Z\) – the cumulants of a categorical indicator – and every order follows from the single rule \[\partial_b\, \pi_a = \pi_a(\delta_{ab} - \pi_b)\] applied repeatedly with the product rule. All four orders are closed form, and one identity follows immediately: differentiating \(\sum_a \pi_a = 1\) shows that every derivative component sums to zero over the value index, at every order.

s <- simplex(4)
eta <- c(0.5, -0.2, 0.8)
round(param_value(s, eta), 4)
#>     p1     p2     p3     p4 
#> 0.2896 0.1438 0.3909 0.1757

# the free identity: each component of each order sums to zero
range(vapply(param_d3(s, eta), sum, numeric(1)))
#> [1] -1.387779e-17  3.903128e-18

A transition matrix of a Markov chain on \(K\) states is a \(K \times K\) matrix whose rows lie on the simplex, and transition_matrix() treats it as exactly that: \(K\) independent additive log-ratio charts, one per row, \(K(K-1)\) free values in all. The independence carries to the derivatives, which are block diagonal by row – a component pairing free values of two different rows is exactly zero, not small – so the cost of an order grows with the row, not with the matrix.

5.2.8 The matrix logarithm

The log-Cholesky map is not the only chart on the positive definite cone. matrix_log() writes \(M = \exp(S)\) with \(S\) symmetric and genuinely free: the free values fill the triangle of \(S\) directly, and no entry is transformed. Two quantities are then exact by construction. The log-determinant is the trace, \[\log\lvert M \rvert = \operatorname{tr}(S),\] linear in the diagonal free values with every higher derivative zero, and the inverse is \(M^{-1} = \exp(-S)\), through the same eigendecomposition that evaluates the map. This is the chart to reach for when the covariance and the precision are wanted at once; log-Cholesky remains the cheaper chart for derivatives, which under it are sparse products.

The derivatives of \(\exp(S)\) in the free values are its Fréchet derivatives, and the package computes them by the representation of Daleckii and Krein (1965): with \(S = Q \Lambda Q^\top\) and the directions rotated by \(Q\), the \(k\)-th derivative contracts chains of directions against divided differences of \(e^x\) of order \(k + 1\) at the eigenvalues, summed over the orderings of the directions. The divided differences themselves are computed by the theorem of Opitz (1964) – the exponential of a small upper bidiagonal matrix, read off its corner entry – which remains exact when eigenvalues repeat or nearly repeat, where the recursive quotient formula cancels catastrophically.

m <- matrix_log(3)
eta <- c(0.2, -0.3, 0.4, 0.5, -0.1, 0.3)

# the log-determinant is the trace of S, read off the free vector
c(param_logdet(m, eta), sum(eta[1:3]))
#> [1] 0.3 0.3

# and the inverse costs no factorization
max(abs(param_solve(m, eta) - solve(param_value(m, eta))))
#> [1] 6.661338e-16

5.2.9 Third and fourth derivatives

Every family above carries its derivatives to fourth order in closed form: log-Cholesky by the Leibniz rule on \(M = LL^\top\), the diagonal and scaled families through the link derivatives that linkfunctions7 already supplies at fourth order, the simplex by its cumulant recursion, and the matrix logarithm by the divided-difference contraction. A parameter defined outside the package is served by numerical fallbacks that apply one product stencil directly to the map for each component – never a stencil on a lower-order numerical derivative – so the accuracy degrades predictably with the order and the no-nesting rule of the toolkit holds structurally.

The higher orders exist for a consumer: the multivariate gaussian of Section 3.5.2 builds its own third and fourth derivatives from these, one Leibniz expansion away, and a mixed-effects model that profiles or corrects its likelihood asks for exactly that.

5.2.10 Which side of a model a family parametrizes

The name of a family does not say whether the matrix it produces is a covariance or a precision, and the two are different models. Imposing a parameter on \(\Sigma\) and imposing the same parameter on \(\Omega = \Sigma^{-1}\) give the same family only when the parameter is closed under inversion. Diagonal matrices are, and so is compound symmetry, whose inverse is again compound symmetry with different constants. An AR(1) correlation matrix is not: its inverse is tridiagonal, which is a different pattern of zeros, so an AR(1) covariance and an AR(1) precision are genuinely different assumptions about the same data.

A parameter therefore carries a label recording which side it is meant for. Nothing computed depends on it and no method reads it; it exists so that the object says what it is, and so that a consumer that cares can ask. The distribution of Section 3.5.2 takes the parameter on either side and adjusts its own derivatives accordingly, which is the only place the difference has arithmetic consequences.

5.3 Rank and the null space

5.3.1 Why a singular matrix is admitted

A covariance matrix must be positive definite for the density it describes to exist, and a precision matrix must be positive definite for the same reason: a singular covariance puts the whole distribution on a subspace, and a singular precision leaves the density unnormalizable. Both readings of a multivariate gaussian therefore require full rank, and the distribution of Section 3.5.2 refuses a rank-deficient parameter.

A penalty is not a density. Writing a quadratic penalty \(\frac{\lambda}{2}\beta^\top P \beta\) as a negative log prior makes it a gaussian with precision \(\lambda P\), and the whole point of a smoothing penalty is that \(P\) is singular: its null space is the set of functions the penalty leaves alone. A second-difference penalty on a spline has the constants and the straight lines in its null space, which is what makes the penalty shrink towards a straight line rather than towards zero. That prior is improper, and its impropriety is the modeling assumption rather than a defect.

The package therefore admits rank-deficient matrices, and everything a consumer asks of one has to be redefined so that it still means something.

5.3.2 The pseudo-determinant

The determinant of a singular matrix is zero, so \(\log|M|\) is \(-\infty\) and useless. The quantity that takes its place is the pseudo-determinant, the product of the non-zero eigenvalues,

\[ \log|M|_{+} \;=\; \sum_{j : \, \nu_j \neq 0} \log \nu_j , \tag{5.9}\]

which is the normalizing constant of the proper gaussian the penalty induces on the complement of its null space. One generic returns both, because a consumer asks the same question of either matrix — what normalizing constant does this contribute — and the object knows which answer is right, through the rank it declares.

The gradient identity Equation 5.2 survives with the Moore-Penrose inverse in place of \(M^{-1}\), and for the scaled family of Section 5.2.3 it collapses to the constant of Equation 5.8.

That constant is what makes the scale estimable. The penalized objective seen as a negative log posterior contributes

\[ \frac{\lambda}{2}\,\beta^\top P \beta \;-\; \frac{r}{2}\log\lambda , \tag{5.10}\]

and setting its derivative in \(\lambda\) to zero gives

\[ \hat{\lambda} = \frac{r}{\beta^\top P \beta}. \tag{5.11}\]

Dropping the second term of Equation 5.10, which is what a treatment that regards the penalty as a penalty rather than as a prior does, leaves a derivative of one sign and sends \(\lambda\) to zero. The normalizing constant is therefore kept, and the rank in Equation 5.11 is the reason the rank has to be right.

dm <- diff(diag(8), differences = 2)
pen <- parameters7::scaled_matrix(crossprod(dm))
p_mat <- crossprod(dm)

set.seed(3)
beta <- as.numeric(rnorm(8))
quad <- drop(t(beta) %*% p_mat %*% beta)

# the stationary point of (lambda/2) b'Pb - (r/2) log lambda, and the same
# point found by minimizing the objective numerically
obj <- function(lambda) 0.5 * lambda * quad - 0.5 * pen@rank * log(lambda)
c(closed_form = pen@rank / quad,
  numerical = stats::optimize(obj, c(1e-8, 1e8))$minimum)
#> closed_form   numerical 
#>   0.4062954   0.4063148

5.3.3 Determining the rank

The rank of a matrix is a statement about the matrix, and a numerical procedure that reads it off an assembled matrix does not always make that statement. Counting eigenvalues above a relative tolerance is the obvious route, and it fails on exactly the object this package exists to carry.

Consider a tensor-product penalty \(\lambda_1 P_1 + \lambda_2 P_2\) on a \(4 \times 8\) grid of coefficients, where \(P_1\) penalizes second differences along one margin and \(P_2\) along the other. Its true rank is 28 out of 32. Counting eigenvalues of the assembled matrix above a relative tolerance gives 28 while the two smoothing parameters are comparable and 24 once they are ten orders of magnitude apart, because the contributions of the smaller term sink below the tolerance and are read as exact zeros. Smoothing parameters that far apart are an ordinary fitted model rather than a pathology, so the count is not a property of the family at all.

What is stable is the null space. The null space of a sum of positive semidefinite matrices is the intersection of their null spaces, since \(\beta^\top(P_1 + P_2)\beta = 0\) with both terms non-negative forces both to vanish. The intersection is the null space of the stacked matrix \(\begin{pmatrix} P_1 \\ P_2 \end{pmatrix}\), so the rank comes from the components, individually normalized and stacked, and never from an assembled combination. Membership of the null space is then tested against the stored basis rather than by counting eigenvalues again.

d2 <- function(k) crossprod(diff(diag(k), differences = 2))
p1 <- kronecker(d2(4), diag(8))
p2 <- kronecker(diag(4), d2(8))

count_ev <- function(m, tol = 1e-10) {
  ev <- eigen(m, symmetric = TRUE, only.values = TRUE)$values
  sum(ev > tol * max(ev))
}

# the stored null basis, and the residual it leaves in the assembled matrix
ns <- parameters7::param_null_basis(list(p1, p2))

ratios <- c(1, 1e4, 1e8, 1e10, 1e12)
data.frame(
  ratio = ratios,
  eigen_count = vapply(ratios, function(f) count_ev(p1 + f * p2), numeric(1)),
  stacked_rank = ns$rank,
  null_residual = vapply(ratios, function(f) {
    m <- p1 + f * p2
    max(abs(m %*% ns$null_basis)) / max(abs(m))
  }, numeric(1))
)
#>   ratio eigen_count stacked_rank null_residual
#> 1 1e+00          28           28  2.176289e-16
#> 2 1e+04          28           28  2.652472e-16
#> 3 1e+08          28           28  2.793968e-16
#> 4 1e+10          24           28  3.099442e-16
#> 5 1e+12          24           28  2.721151e-16

The eigenvalue count varies with the ratio of the smoothing parameters, while the residual \(\lVert M N \rVert / \lVert M \rVert\), with \(N\) the stored basis, stays at the level of rounding across the whole range: the four directions are annihilated by the penalty at every combination of smoothing parameters.

The same doubt applies wherever a Cholesky factorization is used to decide a branch. On a matrix with an exactly zero eigenvalue the pivot that ought to be zero comes out positive or negative according to rounding, so a tryCatch(chol(...)) used as a rank test answers differently on different machines for the same matrix. The verdict must come from the eigenvalues, which describe the matrix, and not from the arithmetic performed on it.

5.3.4 The solve

The remaining quantity is the solve, and it is the one that is refused rather than redefined. A Moore-Penrose solve \(M^{+}B\) exists and is easy to compute, but no consumer of an improper prior asks for it. What a penalized fit inverts is \(X^\top X + \lambda P\), which is non-singular even when \(P\) is not, and which the consumer assembles from the penalty and its own design; what it asks of the penalty itself is the quadratic form \(\beta^\top P \beta\) and the log pseudo-determinant. A pseudo-inverse would answer a question no consumer of an improper prior asks, and a caller that used it in place of the intended solve would obtain wrong results without any signal.

parameters7::param_solve(pen, 0)
#> Error:
#> ! 'scaled' is rank deficient (6 of 8), so it has no inverse. A consumer of
#>   an improper prior needs the quadratic form and the log
#>   pseudo-determinant, not a pseudo-inverse: assemble the matrix the
#>   model actually inverts and solve that.

What the null space is for, instead, is membership. A basis of it is stored at construction, so a consumer can ask whether a coefficient vector lies in the space the penalty leaves alone — for a second-difference penalty, whether it is a straight line — without touching an eigenvalue.

n_basis <- pen@null_basis
line <- 1 + 2 * seq_len(8)
quad_of <- function(b) drop(t(b) %*% p_mat %*% b)

c(
  columns = ncol(n_basis),
  penalty_of_a_line = quad_of(line),
  penalty_of_a_curve = quad_of(seq_len(8)^2),
  line_is_in_the_null_space =
    max(abs(line - n_basis %*% crossprod(n_basis, line))) < 1e-10
)
#>                   columns         penalty_of_a_line        penalty_of_a_curve 
#>                         2                         0                        24 
#> line_is_in_the_null_space 
#>                         1
