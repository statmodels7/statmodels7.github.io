9  The statmodels7 package

The chapters so far built the parts a regression model is made of: the links of Chapter 2, the distributions of Chapter 3, the optimizers of Chapter 4, the constrained parameters of Chapter 5, the bases of Chapter 6, the penalties of Chapter 7 and the model terms of Chapter 8. statmodels7 is the layer that fits a model out of them.

What it fits is a distributional regression. An ordinary regression writes a linear predictor for the mean and holds the rest of the distribution constant across observations; here every parameter of the response distribution carries an equation of its own, so a scale, a shape or a zero-inflation probability is modeled by the same machinery and with the same terms as a mean. One formula carries all of those equations, the terms they name are assembled into a penalized likelihood, and the smoothing parameters and prior scales the terms leave free are estimated from the data.

Section 9.1 presents the model: the formula with its equations separated by bars, the scale each equation is written on, the specification built from it, and what a fit keeps. Section 9.2 presents the penalized fit: the objective, the scoring step and the augmented decomposition it is solved through, the blocks that move with their own coefficients, the blocks whose penalty has a kink and are fitted apart, and the rule that ends the loop.

9.1 The model

A response distribution with \(K\) parameters is written here with an equation for each of them. Observation \(i\) has its own parameter vector \(\theta_i = (\theta_{i1}, \ldots, \theta_{iK})\), and each coordinate is carried onto the whole real line by that parameter’s own link, exactly as Section 3.1.4 carries it for a single distribution:

\[ g_k(\theta_{ik}) \;=\; \eta_{ik} \;=\; o_{ik} + x_{ik}'\beta_k , \qquad k = 1, \ldots, K . \]

Here \(x_{ik}'\) is the \(i\)th row of the \(k\)th equation’s design \(X_k\), which is the blocks of the terms that equation names set side by side, and \(\beta_k\) the coefficients they carry; \(o_{ik}\) is an offset, a column of the predictor whose coefficient is known to be one. The log-likelihood is the weighted sum of the family’s own,

\[ \ell(\beta) \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i \, \log f\!\left(y_i; \theta_i(\beta)\right), \]

with the prior weights \(w_i\) taken as given and never normalized. Dividing by their sum would turn the log-likelihood into a mean, which shrinks every standard error by \(\sqrt{n}\) and makes an information criterion incomparable with the unweighted fit of the same model.

Writing an equation for every parameter rather than for the mean alone is the distributional regression of Rigby and Stasinopoulos (2005); what differs here is that the terms those equations may name are the whole of Chapter 8 rather than a fixed catalog, and that the parameters they are written for are whatever the family of Chapter 3 declares.

9.1.1 The formula

The equations are separated by a bar, the first carrying the response:

y ~ x1 + s(x2) + ridge(R)  |  sigma ~ z  |  nu ~ 1

The first equation belongs to the family’s first parameter and each bar introduces the next. The parameters may be named in any order, and one with no equation of its own is given ~ 1, an intercept, so a formula with no bar at all fits an ordinary regression with every other parameter constant.

Recovering those equations is not a matter of splitting the right-hand side. R’s ~ binds looser than | and associates to the left, so

y ~ a | sigma ~ b | nu ~ c

parses as ((y ~ (a | sigma)) ~ (b | nu)) ~ c, whose right-hand side is c alone: splitting it on | returns one piece and drops two equations without an error. What the associativity calls for is a walk down the left spine of the nested ~ calls, collecting each level’s right-hand side and reversing at the end, with the innermost left operand as the response. The walk descends through ~ and through a | at the top of a collected piece, and through nothing else, so a bar written inside a call is left alone and random(1 | id) reaches its constructor whole.

eq <- statmod_equations(y ~ a | sigma ~ b | nu ~ cc,
                        c("mu", "sigma", "nu"))
deparse(eq$response)
#> [1] "y"
vapply(eq$equations, function(q) deparse(q[[2]]), character(1))
#>    mu sigma    nu 
#>   "a"   "b"  "cc"

The same call on a formula that names one parameter fills the others in:

vapply(statmod_equations(y ~ x, c("mu", "sigma", "nu"))$equations,
       function(q) deparse(q[[2]]), character(1))
#>    mu sigma    nu 
#>   "x"   "1"   "1"

Each equation is then read by modelterms7’s interpreter, whose recognition rule is the one of Section 8.3: a call whose value inherits from the term class is a term, and everything else is a covariate, so the bare covariates of an equation collapse into a single parametric block. The interpretation happens in an environment where the term constructors sit in front of the search path, which is what makes s() this toolkit’s smooth even in a session with mgcv attached.

An offset() written among an equation’s terms is taken out before the interpreter sees the rest, and its expression is kept rather than its value, which is what lets an offset survive prediction at new data. One written inside a term’s own formula is rejected rather than moved: in a penalized term’s formula it would be a contribution to the equation’s predictor, and in a subformula a contribution to that parameter’s own chart, and choosing one reading would fit the wrong model in half the cases.

9.1.2 The specification

statmod_spec() performs everything above and stops short of fitting: it splits the formula, interprets each equation, and builds the terms against the data, giving one object that carries the equations, the built terms, the response, the weights and the offsets.

set.seed(9)
dd <- data.frame(x = runif(120, -2, 2), z = runif(120, -2, 2))
dd$y <- rnorm(120, 1 + 0.8 * dd$x, exp(-0.5 + 0.3 * dd$z))

spec <- statmod_spec(y ~ x + s(z, k = 6) | sigma ~ z,
                     gaussian1_distrib(), dd)
names(spec@equations)
#> [1] "mu"    "sigma"
vapply(spec@terms, function(tm) paste(names(tm), collapse = ", "),
       character(1))
#>                    mu                 sigma 
#> "linpar, s(z, k = 6)"              "linpar"

statmod_design() assembles from it, per parameter, the terms’ blocks side by side, together with the names of the coefficients they carry and the range of columns each term occupies:

des <- statmod_design(spec)
vapply(des, function(e) e$npar, integer(1))
#>    mu sigma 
#>     7     2
des$mu$coef_names
#> [1] "(Intercept)" "x"           "s(z).lin"    "s(z).z1"     "s(z).z2"    
#> [6] "s(z).z3"     "s(z).z4"

The mean’s equation carries an intercept and a slope from its parametric block, and from the smooth a linear column together with the reparametrized basis of Section 6.4.4, one column fewer than the basis dimension it was given; the scale’s equation carries an intercept and a slope. The block a term contributes is sparse whenever the term builds it that way – a grouping indicator has density \(1/m\) over \(m\) groups – and an equation’s design is sparse when any one of its blocks is, sparsity being a property of the assembled matrix and never of its pieces.

A fitted model keeps this specification rather than the data. That is what makes prediction at new data a matter of reapplying each term’s blueprint, with the knots of a smooth and the levels of a factor the ones the model was fitted with, instead of rebuilding a basis against rows the fit never saw.

9.1.3 The intercept and a structural level

Most terms add columns to a predictor. A structural term of Section 8.2 rewrites the likelihood instead, and one of them, the score-driven filter, contributes a level that evolves with the data:

\[ f_t \;=\; \omega + \sum_{r=1}^{p} a_r\, s_{t-r} + \sum_{j=1}^{q} b_j\, f_{t-j} , \]

added to whatever the equation’s design already produces. A constant in that same equation and the level’s own \(\omega\) are then exactly confounded. Replacing the intercept by \(\alpha + c\) and \(\omega\) by \(\omega - c\,(1 - \sum_j b_j)\) leaves the recursion’s stationary level lower by exactly \(c\) and every predictor unchanged, because the recursion is affine in the level given the score path and the score path depends on the predictor alone. A fit written that way is not identified: moving along that direction changes neither a predictor nor the likelihood.

Where an equation’s design spans the constant and it also carries a structural term, the intercept keeps the level and the term’s own parameter is held at zero. What is given up by holding \(\omega\) is a quantity the intercept already reports, while the loadings and the persistence, which a constant cannot express, stay free. The question is asked of the span of the equation’s design rather than of a column called (Intercept), since a factor coded without one still sums to the constant.

9.2 The penalized fit

With the hyperparameters held, what is minimized is the negative log-likelihood plus the penalties the terms declare:

\[ \mathcal{O}(\beta; \vartheta) \;=\; -\sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i \, \log f\!\left(y_i; \theta_i(\beta)\right) \;+\; \sum_{k} \rho_k(\beta; \vartheta_k) . \]

Neither part is averaged. A penalty is a negative log-prior, as Section 7.1 sets it out, and a posterior adds a log-likelihood and a log-prior at full size; dividing one of them by \(n\) would leave \(\vartheta\) meaning something that depends on the sample size, so that the same prior scale would describe different beliefs at \(n = 10^2\) and at \(n = 10^5\). What is scaled instead is the rule that stops the loop. The fit of a single distribution in Section 3.3 divides the objective by \(n\) and compares an unscaled threshold against it; here the objective is left alone and the threshold reads a quantity that carries no sample size, and in both places the thing being arranged is that a tolerance mean the same at \(n = 10\) and at \(n = 10^7\).

9.2.1 Two kinds of block

The terms divide by a property each of them already reports. A penalty whose kink set, in the sense of Section 7.2, is empty is twice differentiable in the coefficients, so its block has a curvature and can enter a system solved with one. Everything of that kind goes into a single system and is estimated all at once: an unpenalized block, a ridge, a spline, a random effect, a structured or additive penalty. Their joint curvature exists, and using it is what closes a fit in a handful of iterations.

A penalty with a kink – a lasso, a SCAD, an MCP – has no second derivative at the point its own mode sits on, and its block is estimated by a method of its own with everything else held fixed, through the proximal operator of Section 7.2. The fit alternates between the two until the objective and every block stop moving.

The split is a question put to the penalty and not a list of term names, so a term whose penalty is replaced by a smooth approximation moves into the first group with nothing here to change.

9.2.2 The scoring step

Write \(\Omega_i\) for the \(K \times K\) information of observation \(i\) with respect to its own \(K\) predictors, multiplied by that observation’s weight, and \(Z\) for the design that stacks the equations’ blocks. The step solves

\[ \left(Z' \Omega Z + S(\vartheta)\right) \delta \;=\; Z'g - S(\vartheta)\beta , \]

with \(g\) the score with respect to the predictors and \(S\) the penalties’ Hessian. Taking \(\Omega_i\) to be the expected information makes this Fisher scoring, and the blocks are then positive definite wherever the family is regular, so a step is usable from a poor starting point. Taking the observed Hessian makes it Newton, which converges faster near the optimum and is the curvature an exact gradient of the marginal criterion is written against.

The two matrices are never assembled. Each \(\Omega_i\) has a Cholesky factor \(L_i L_i' = \Omega_i\), computed by writing the recursion out over the \(K\) indices and evaluating each entry over all \(n\) observations at once, so no loop runs over the sample; stacking those factors against the design gives \(R\) with \(R'R = Z'\Omega Z\). The penalty has a factor \(C\) with \(C'C = S\), taken from an eigendecomposition with the non-positive directions dropped, which is what a penalty of deficient rank requires: a spline’s is deficient by exactly the dimension of its null space, and a Cholesky has nothing to return there. The step is then a decomposition of

\[ A \;=\; \begin{bmatrix} R \\ C \end{bmatrix}, \qquad A'A \;=\; Z'\Omega Z + S , \]

and neither cross-product is formed. A design whose condition number is \(\kappa\) is decomposed at \(\kappa\), where forming the normal equations would present the decomposition with \(\kappa^2\). That matters most where the design is worst conditioned by construction: the working design of a break-point term is bounded only to \(\epsilon^{-1/2}\), so squaring it exhausts double precision.

A diagonal penalty is factored by taking the square root of its diagonal, which is the same answer the eigendecomposition gives, the eigenvalues of a diagonal matrix being its diagonal entries. The case is worth detecting for how often it arises: a ridge is diagonal, a random effect is diagonal, and the Demmler-Reinsch penalty of Section 8.2 is \(\operatorname{diag}(0, 1, \ldots, 1)\) exactly, as is any block-diagonal assembly of them.

Where the objective is quadratic in \(\beta\) the whole loop is one solve, and the answer is available in closed form to compare against. Holding the scale of a Gaussian family fixed does exactly that:

set.seed(20260828)
n_ridge <- 80
d_ridge <- data.frame(x = runif(n_ridge, -2, 2))
d_ridge$R <- matrix(rnorm(n_ridge * 3), n_ridge, 3)
d_ridge$y <- 1 + 0.8 * d_ridge$x +
  as.numeric(d_ridge$R %*% c(0.6, -0.4, 0)) + rnorm(n_ridge, sd = 0.7)

lam <- 2.5
ridge_fit <- statmod(y ~ x + ridge(R, lambda = lam),
                     fixed(gaussian1_distrib(), sigma = 1), d_ridge)

Zr <- cbind(1, d_ridge$x, d_ridge$R)
Sr <- diag(c(0, 0, rep(lam, 3)))
closed <- as.numeric(solve(crossprod(Zr) + Sr, crossprod(Zr, d_ridge$y)))

cbind(fitted = unname(coef(ridge_fit)$mu), closed = closed)
#>           fitted      closed
#> [1,]  1.18344424  1.18344424
#> [2,]  0.81881562  0.81881562
#> [3,]  0.64930384  0.64930384
#> [4,] -0.42362693 -0.42362693
#> [5,]  0.03162356  0.03162356

The ridge’s hyperparameter is a precision, so it enters that solve as it stands rather than as one over a square, and the intercept and the slope are unpenalized, which is why the penalty matrix carries two zeros.

9.2.3 The rank of the augmented matrix

A decomposition has to say whether the matrix it was handed is of full rank, and the test reads the diagonal of the triangular factor divided by each column’s norm. Since \(A'A = R'R\), scaling the columns of \(A\) scales that diagonal by the same factors, so the ratio is the diagonal of a decomposition whose columns have unit norm.

Reading the raw diagonal instead reports a matrix as near-singular whenever its columns differ in size, which is what a large smoothing parameter does to the penalty rows of its own block standing beside an unpenalized one. Those matrices are not near-singular; per-column scaling forgives a separation of scale from any source, while an exact collinearity stays exactly singular under it. The distinction is worth a measurement: on a model with a random intercept, the raw test rejected 87 of 127 solves that the dense route finds at full rank, and the equilibrated ratio over the same solves stayed between 0.445 and 1.

Sparsity survives the whole construction. A grouping indicator has density \(1/m\) over \(m\) groups, the square-root design built from it is sparse, and the augmented matrix is decomposed by a sparse QR, which is a QR and keeps the conditioning property the augmented form was chosen for. A sparse Cholesky of the normal equations would be faster still and would give that property up.

9.2.4 Blocks that move with their coefficients

A design block is usually a function of the data alone. The nonlinear term and the break-point terms of Section 8.2 are not: their block is the Jacobian of the contribution at the current coefficients, so it moves as the fit moves. Writing \(f(\beta)\) for what such a term contributes and \(X(\beta)\) for its block, the difference

\[ \mathrm{adj} \;=\; f(\beta) - X(\beta)\,\beta \]

is added to the predictor as a per-observation adjustment. Every cross-product in the step then reads the block as it stands, and the increment the step returns is the Gauss-Newton one for that term. The arrangement covers both constructions with no branch. For a continuous break-point the adjustment is not zero and the iteration is Gauss-Newton; for a discontinuous one the columns satisfy \(X\beta = f\) exactly, by the identity that writes an indicator as \(\tfrac12 + (x - \psi)/(2\lvert x - \psi\rvert)\), so the adjustment vanishes and the same step is the working fit of Fasola, Muggeo, and Küchenhoff (2018).

Such a block is refreshed once per sweep rather than once per trial point. A discontinuous term’s rescaling factor halves whenever its break-point reverses direction, which is a fact about the path and not about the point: refreshing it inside a line search would advance that schedule at every trial the search rejects, and refreshing it from the specification would freeze it at its starting value and solve a permanently smoothed problem instead.

9.2.5 When the loop stops

The rule that drives the loop compares the score per observation, \(\max_j \lvert g_j \rvert / n\), against a tolerance, so that the threshold means the same at \(n = 10\) and at \(n = 10^7\). Any criterion object of Chapter 4 may drive it instead, and since the state’s gradient is that same per-observation score, crit_grad(t) is exactly the tolerance \(t\).

The verdict a fit reports is arbitrated by a second reading, dimensionless in the response’s units. Writing \(\mathcal{J}_p\) for the coordinates equation \(p\) owns and \(H\) for the information’s diagonal, it is

\[ \max_{j \in \mathcal{J}_p} \frac{\lvert g_j \rvert}{n\, s_p}, \qquad s_p = \sqrt{\operatorname{median}_{j \in \mathcal{J}_p} H_{jj} / n} , \]

read per equation and maximized over them. The score of a location equation carries the units \(1/y\) and its curvature \(1/y^2\), so the ratio survives any rescaling of the response, which the absolute rule alone does not. This reading can relabel a run that stalled at the optimum as converged and can never stop one that is still moving; driving the loop with it was tried and fails at large \(y\), where the guard on a stalled objective fires before a rule \(1/s_p\) stricter can.

A term whose block is a frozen working linearization answers the question for itself. The gradient the layer computes there belongs to the working model, and the profile objective of a discontinuous break-point term is a step function in the position with no gradient to vanish, so what ends that fit is the term’s own report that its construction has reached a fixed point.
