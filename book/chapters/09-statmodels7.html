9  The statmodels7 package

The chapters so far built the parts a regression model is made of: the links of Chapter 2, the distributions of Chapter 3, the optimizers of Chapter 4, the constrained parameters of Chapter 5, the bases of Chapter 6, the penalties of Chapter 7 and the model terms of Chapter 8. statmodels7 is the layer that fits a model out of them.

What it fits is a distributional regression. An ordinary regression writes a linear predictor for the mean and holds the rest of the distribution constant across observations; here every parameter of the response distribution carries an equation of its own, so a scale, a shape or a zero-inflation probability is modeled by the same machinery and with the same terms as a mean. One formula carries all of those equations, the terms they name are assembled into a penalized likelihood, and the smoothing parameters and prior scales the terms leave free are estimated from the data.

Section 9.1 presents the model: the formula with its equations separated by bars, the scale each equation is written on, the specification built from it, and what a fit keeps. Section 9.2 presents the penalized fit: the objective, the scoring step and the augmented decomposition it is solved through, the blocks that move with their own coefficients, the blocks whose penalty has a kink and are fitted apart, and the rule that ends the loop. Section 9.3 presents the second estimation problem sitting outside the first, the hyperparameters the terms leave free: the marginal criteria and their exact derivatives, the prediction-error criteria, the path a penalty with a kink is swept along, and the effective degrees of freedom a criterion is charged against. Section 9.4 presents the terms that rewrite the likelihood rather than adding to it, and what a fit has to compute for them. Section 9.5 presents the two questions that bracket a fit, where it begins and whether the point it reports is the one the model asks for. Section 9.6 presents what a fit answers when it is read: the two variances and the intervals built on them, which likelihood is reported, prediction at new data, and simulation from the model. Section 9.7 closes the chapter with two models fitted and read from end to end.

9.1 The model

A response distribution with \(K\) parameters is written here with an equation for each of them. Observation \(i\) has its own parameter vector \(\theta_i = (\theta_{i1}, \ldots, \theta_{iK})\), and each coordinate is carried onto the whole real line by that parameter’s own link, exactly as Section 3.1.4 carries it for a single distribution:

\[ g_k(\theta_{ik}) \;=\; \eta_{ik} \;=\; o_{ik} + x_{ik}'\beta_k , \qquad k = 1, \ldots, K . \]

Here \(x_{ik}'\) is the \(i\)th row of the \(k\)th equation’s design \(X_k\), which is the blocks of the terms that equation names set side by side, and \(\beta_k\) the coefficients they carry; \(o_{ik}\) is an offset, a column of the predictor whose coefficient is known to be one. The log-likelihood is the weighted sum of the family’s own,

\[ \ell(\beta) \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i \, \log f\!\left(y_i; \theta_i(\beta)\right), \]

with the prior weights \(w_i\) taken as given and never normalized. Dividing by their sum would turn the log-likelihood into a mean, which shrinks every standard error by \(\sqrt{n}\) and makes an information criterion incomparable with the unweighted fit of the same model.

Writing an equation for every parameter rather than for the mean alone is the distributional regression of Rigby and Stasinopoulos (2005); what differs here is that the terms those equations may name are the whole of Chapter 8 rather than a fixed catalog, and that the parameters they are written for are whatever the family of Chapter 3 declares.

9.1.1 The formula

The equations are separated by a bar, the first carrying the response:

y ~ x1 + s(x2) + ridge(R)  |  sigma ~ z  |  nu ~ 1

The first equation belongs to the family’s first parameter and each bar introduces the next. The parameters may be named in any order, and one with no equation of its own is given ~ 1, an intercept, so a formula with no bar at all fits an ordinary regression with every other parameter constant.

Recovering those equations is not a matter of splitting the right-hand side. R’s ~ binds looser than | and associates to the left, so

y ~ a | sigma ~ b | nu ~ c

parses as ((y ~ (a | sigma)) ~ (b | nu)) ~ c, whose right-hand side is c alone: splitting it on | returns one piece and drops two equations without an error. What the associativity calls for is a walk down the left spine of the nested ~ calls, collecting each level’s right-hand side and reversing at the end, with the innermost left operand as the response. The walk descends through ~ and through a | at the top of a collected piece, and through nothing else, so a bar written inside a call is left alone and random(1 | id) reaches its constructor whole.

eq <- statmod_equations(y ~ a | sigma ~ b | nu ~ cc,
                        c("mu", "sigma", "nu"))
deparse(eq$response)
#> [1] "y"
vapply(eq$equations, function(q) deparse(q[[2]]), character(1))
#>    mu sigma    nu 
#>   "a"   "b"  "cc"

The same call on a formula that names one parameter fills the others in:

vapply(statmod_equations(y ~ x, c("mu", "sigma", "nu"))$equations,
       function(q) deparse(q[[2]]), character(1))
#>    mu sigma    nu 
#>   "x"   "1"   "1"

Each equation is then read by modelterms7’s interpreter, whose recognition rule is the one of Section 8.3: a call whose value inherits from the term class is a term, and everything else is a covariate, so the bare covariates of an equation collapse into a single parametric block. The interpretation happens in an environment where the term constructors sit in front of the search path, which is what makes s() this toolkit’s smooth even in a session with mgcv attached.

An offset() written among an equation’s terms is taken out before the interpreter sees the rest, and its expression is kept rather than its value, which is what lets an offset survive prediction at new data. One written inside a term’s own formula is rejected rather than moved: in a penalized term’s formula it would be a contribution to the equation’s predictor, and in a subformula a contribution to that parameter’s own chart, and choosing one reading would fit the wrong model in half the cases.

9.1.2 The specification

statmod_spec() performs everything above and stops short of fitting: it splits the formula, interprets each equation, and builds the terms against the data, giving one object that carries the equations, the built terms, the response, the weights and the offsets.

set.seed(9)
dd <- data.frame(x = runif(120, -2, 2), z = runif(120, -2, 2))
dd$y <- rnorm(120, 1 + 0.8 * dd$x, exp(-0.5 + 0.3 * dd$z))

spec <- statmod_spec(y ~ x + s(z, k = 6) | sigma ~ z,
                     gaussian1_distrib(), dd)
names(spec@equations)
#> [1] "mu"    "sigma"
vapply(spec@terms, function(tm) paste(names(tm), collapse = ", "),
       character(1))
#>                    mu                 sigma 
#> "linpar, s(z, k = 6)"              "linpar"

statmod_design() assembles from it, per parameter, the terms’ blocks side by side, together with the names of the coefficients they carry and the range of columns each term occupies:

des <- statmod_design(spec)
vapply(des, function(e) e$npar, integer(1))
#>    mu sigma 
#>     7     2
des$mu$coef_names
#> [1] "(Intercept)" "x"           "s(z).lin"    "s(z).z1"     "s(z).z2"    
#> [6] "s(z).z3"     "s(z).z4"

The mean’s equation carries an intercept and a slope from its parametric block, and from the smooth a linear column together with the reparametrized basis of Section 6.4.4, one column fewer than the basis dimension it was given; the scale’s equation carries an intercept and a slope. The block a term contributes is sparse whenever the term builds it that way – a grouping indicator has density \(1/m\) over \(m\) groups – and an equation’s design is sparse when any one of its blocks is, sparsity being a property of the assembled matrix and never of its pieces.

A fitted model keeps this specification rather than the data. That is what makes prediction at new data a matter of reapplying each term’s blueprint, with the knots of a smooth and the levels of a factor the ones the model was fitted with, instead of rebuilding a basis against rows the fit never saw.

9.1.3 The intercept and a structural level

Most terms add columns to a predictor. A structural term of Section 8.2 rewrites the likelihood instead, and one of them, the score-driven filter, contributes a level that evolves with the data:

\[ f_t \;=\; \omega + \sum_{r=1}^{p} a_r\, s_{t-r} + \sum_{j=1}^{q} b_j\, f_{t-j} , \]

added to whatever the equation’s design already produces. A constant in that same equation and the level’s own \(\omega\) are then exactly confounded. Replacing the intercept by \(\alpha + c\) and \(\omega\) by \(\omega - c\,(1 - \sum_j b_j)\) leaves the recursion’s stationary level lower by exactly \(c\) and every predictor unchanged, because the recursion is affine in the level given the score path and the score path depends on the predictor alone. A fit written that way is not identified: moving along that direction changes neither a predictor nor the likelihood.

Where an equation’s design spans the constant and it also carries a structural term, the intercept keeps the level and the term’s own parameter is held at zero. What is given up by holding \(\omega\) is a quantity the intercept already reports, while the loadings and the persistence, which a constant cannot express, stay free. The question is asked of the span of the equation’s design rather than of a column called (Intercept), since a factor coded without one still sums to the constant.

9.2 The penalized fit

With the hyperparameters held, what is minimized is the negative log-likelihood plus the penalties the terms declare:

\[ \mathcal{O}(\beta; \vartheta) \;=\; -\sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i \, \log f\!\left(y_i; \theta_i(\beta)\right) \;+\; \sum_{k} \rho_k(\beta; \vartheta_k) . \]

Neither part is averaged. A penalty is a negative log-prior, as Section 7.1 sets it out, and a posterior adds a log-likelihood and a log-prior at full size; dividing one of them by \(n\) would leave \(\vartheta\) meaning something that depends on the sample size, so that the same prior scale would describe different beliefs at \(n = 10^2\) and at \(n = 10^5\). What is scaled instead is the rule that stops the loop. The fit of a single distribution in Section 3.3 divides the objective by \(n\) and compares an unscaled threshold against it; here the objective is left alone and the threshold reads a quantity that carries no sample size, and in both places the thing being arranged is that a tolerance mean the same at \(n = 10\) and at \(n = 10^7\).

9.2.1 Two kinds of block

The terms divide by a property each of them already reports. A penalty whose kink set, in the sense of Section 7.2, is empty is twice differentiable in the coefficients, so its block has a curvature and can enter a system solved with one. Everything of that kind goes into a single system and is estimated all at once: an unpenalized block, a ridge, a spline, a random effect, a structured or additive penalty. Their joint curvature exists, and using it is what closes a fit in a handful of iterations.

A penalty with a kink – a lasso, a SCAD, an MCP – has no second derivative at the point its own mode sits on, and its block is estimated by a method of its own with everything else held fixed, through the proximal operator of Section 7.2. The fit alternates between the two until the objective and every block stop moving.

The split is a question put to the penalty and not a list of term names, so a term whose penalty is replaced by a smooth approximation moves into the first group with nothing here to change.

9.2.2 The scoring step

Write \(\Omega_i\) for the \(K \times K\) information of observation \(i\) with respect to its own \(K\) predictors, multiplied by that observation’s weight, and \(Z\) for the design that stacks the equations’ blocks. The step solves

\[ \left(Z' \Omega Z + S(\vartheta)\right) \delta \;=\; Z'g - S(\vartheta)\beta , \]

with \(g\) the score with respect to the predictors and \(S\) the penalties’ Hessian. Taking \(\Omega_i\) to be the expected information makes this Fisher scoring, and the blocks are then positive definite wherever the family is regular, so a step is usable from a poor starting point. Taking the observed Hessian makes it Newton, which converges faster near the optimum and is the curvature an exact gradient of the marginal criterion is written against.

The two matrices are never assembled. Each \(\Omega_i\) has a Cholesky factor \(L_i L_i' = \Omega_i\), computed by writing the recursion out over the \(K\) indices and evaluating each entry over all \(n\) observations at once, so no loop runs over the sample; stacking those factors against the design gives \(R\) with \(R'R = Z'\Omega Z\). The penalty has a factor \(C\) with \(C'C = S\), taken from an eigendecomposition with the non-positive directions dropped, which is what a penalty of deficient rank requires: a spline’s is deficient by exactly the dimension of its null space, and a Cholesky has nothing to return there. The step is then a decomposition of

\[ A \;=\; \begin{bmatrix} R \\ C \end{bmatrix}, \qquad A'A \;=\; Z'\Omega Z + S , \]

and neither cross-product is formed. A design whose condition number is \(\kappa\) is decomposed at \(\kappa\), where forming the normal equations would present the decomposition with \(\kappa^2\). That matters most where the design is worst conditioned by construction: the working design of a break-point term is bounded only to \(\epsilon^{-1/2}\), so squaring it exhausts double precision.

A diagonal penalty is factored by taking the square root of its diagonal, which is the same answer the eigendecomposition gives, the eigenvalues of a diagonal matrix being its diagonal entries. The case is worth detecting for how often it arises: a ridge is diagonal, a random effect is diagonal, and the Demmler-Reinsch penalty of Section 8.2 is \(\operatorname{diag}(0, 1, \ldots, 1)\) exactly, as is any block-diagonal assembly of them.

Where the objective is quadratic in \(\beta\) the whole loop is one solve, and the answer is available in closed form to compare against. Holding the scale of a Gaussian family fixed does exactly that:

set.seed(20260828)
n_ridge <- 80
d_ridge <- data.frame(x = runif(n_ridge, -2, 2))
d_ridge$R <- matrix(rnorm(n_ridge * 3), n_ridge, 3)
d_ridge$y <- 1 + 0.8 * d_ridge$x +
  as.numeric(d_ridge$R %*% c(0.6, -0.4, 0)) + rnorm(n_ridge, sd = 0.7)

lam <- 2.5
ridge_fit <- statmod(y ~ x + ridge(R, lambda = lam),
                     fixed(gaussian1_distrib(), sigma = 1), d_ridge)

Zr <- cbind(1, d_ridge$x, d_ridge$R)
Sr <- diag(c(0, 0, rep(lam, 3)))
closed <- as.numeric(solve(crossprod(Zr) + Sr, crossprod(Zr, d_ridge$y)))

cbind(fitted = unname(coef(ridge_fit)$mu), closed = closed)
#>           fitted      closed
#> [1,]  1.18344424  1.18344424
#> [2,]  0.81881562  0.81881562
#> [3,]  0.64930384  0.64930384
#> [4,] -0.42362693 -0.42362693
#> [5,]  0.03162356  0.03162356

The ridge’s hyperparameter is a precision, so it enters that solve as it stands rather than as one over a square, and the intercept and the slope are unpenalized, which is why the penalty matrix carries two zeros.

9.2.3 The rank of the augmented matrix

A decomposition has to say whether the matrix it was handed is of full rank, and the test reads the diagonal of the triangular factor divided by each column’s norm. Since \(A'A = R'R\), scaling the columns of \(A\) scales that diagonal by the same factors, so the ratio is the diagonal of a decomposition whose columns have unit norm.

Reading the raw diagonal instead reports a matrix as near-singular whenever its columns differ in size, which is what a large smoothing parameter does to the penalty rows of its own block standing beside an unpenalized one. Those matrices are not near-singular; per-column scaling forgives a separation of scale from any source, while an exact collinearity stays exactly singular under it. The distinction is worth a measurement: on a model with a random intercept, the raw test rejected 87 of 127 solves that the dense route finds at full rank, and the equilibrated ratio over the same solves stayed between 0.445 and 1.

Sparsity survives the whole construction. A grouping indicator has density \(1/m\) over \(m\) groups, the square-root design built from it is sparse, and the augmented matrix is decomposed by a sparse QR, which is a QR and keeps the conditioning property the augmented form was chosen for. A sparse Cholesky of the normal equations would be faster still and would give that property up.

9.2.4 Blocks that move with their coefficients

A design block is usually a function of the data alone. The nonlinear term and the break-point terms of Section 8.2 are not: their block is the Jacobian of the contribution at the current coefficients, so it moves as the fit moves. Writing \(f(\beta)\) for what such a term contributes and \(X(\beta)\) for its block, the difference

\[ \mathrm{adj} \;=\; f(\beta) - X(\beta)\,\beta \]

is added to the predictor as a per-observation adjustment. Every cross-product in the step then reads the block as it stands, and the increment the step returns is the Gauss-Newton one for that term. The arrangement covers both constructions with no branch. For a continuous break-point the adjustment is not zero and the iteration is Gauss-Newton; for a discontinuous one the columns satisfy \(X\beta = f\) exactly, by the identity that writes an indicator as \(\tfrac12 + (x - \psi)/(2\lvert x - \psi\rvert)\), so the adjustment vanishes and the same step is the working fit of Fasola, Muggeo, and Küchenhoff (2018).

Such a block is refreshed once per sweep rather than once per trial point. A discontinuous term’s rescaling factor halves whenever its break-point reverses direction, which is a fact about the path and not about the point: refreshing it inside a line search would advance that schedule at every trial the search rejects, and refreshing it from the specification would freeze it at its starting value and solve a permanently smoothed problem instead.

9.2.5 When the loop stops

The rule that drives the loop compares the score per observation, \(\max_j \lvert g_j \rvert / n\), against a tolerance, so that the threshold means the same at \(n = 10\) and at \(n = 10^7\). Any criterion object of Chapter 4 may drive it instead, and since the state’s gradient is that same per-observation score, crit_grad(t) is exactly the tolerance \(t\).

The verdict a fit reports is arbitrated by a second reading, dimensionless in the response’s units. Writing \(\mathcal{J}_p\) for the coordinates equation \(p\) owns and \(H\) for the information’s diagonal, it is

\[ \max_{j \in \mathcal{J}_p} \frac{\lvert g_j \rvert}{n\, s_p}, \qquad s_p = \sqrt{\operatorname{median}_{j \in \mathcal{J}_p} H_{jj} / n} , \]

read per equation and maximized over them. The score of a location equation carries the units \(1/y\) and its curvature \(1/y^2\), so the ratio survives any rescaling of the response, which the absolute rule alone does not. This reading can relabel a run that stalled at the optimum as converged and can never stop one that is still moving; driving the loop with it was tried and fails at large \(y\), where the guard on a stalled objective fires before a rule \(1/s_p\) stricter can.

A term whose block is a frozen working linearization answers the question for itself. The gradient the layer computes there belongs to the working model, and the profile objective of a discontinuous break-point term is a step function in the position with no gradient to vanish, so what ends that fit is the term’s own report that its construction has reached a fixed point.

9.3 Choosing the hyperparameters

A penalty carries hyperparameters: a smoothing parameter, the scale of a prior, the \(\lambda\) and \(\alpha\) of an elastic net. A term holds any of them it was given a value for, and every one left free is estimated from the same data the coefficients are. That is a second estimation problem sitting outside the first, and the model is refitted at every value it visits.

Two families of criterion answer it, and they ask different questions. A marginal criterion integrates the coefficients out and asks which hyperparameter makes the data most likely; a prediction-error criterion asks which one predicts best. For a smooth penalty either applies. For a penalty with a kink only the second does, and the reason is the subject of the last part of this section.

9.3.1 The marginal criterion

The coefficients are integrated out by a Laplace approximation at the penalized mode. Writing \(\hat\beta(\theta)\) for that mode, \(H\) for the information of the log-likelihood, \(S\) for the penalty’s second derivative in the coefficients and \(A\) for an orthonormal basis of the subspace integrated over,

\[ \log L(\theta) \;=\; \ell(\hat\beta) - \rho(\hat\beta; \theta) + \frac{q}{2}\log 2\pi - \frac12 \log\bigl\lvert A'(H+S)A \bigr\rvert , \]

with \(q\) the dimension of that subspace. Nothing is added to \(\rho\) to make this work. A penalties7 penalty keeps its normalizing constant, as Section 7.1 sets out, so \(\rho\) is exactly minus a log prior density, and for a quadratic penalty that constant carries the \(-\frac{r}{2}\log\lambda\) and the log pseudo-determinant of Section 7.3 that a marginal criterion needs. Written out, the expression reproduces the REML criterion of Wood (2011) term for term.

The two members differ in what they integrate. reml() takes \(A = I\): every coefficient is integrated, the unpenalized ones under the flat prior their absence of a penalty amounts to. ml() takes \(A\) spanning the range of the penalty, so an unpenalized direction is profiled instead — an ordinary covariate is one, and so is the linear column of a Demmler-Reinsch smooth, which that smooth’s penalty leaves alone. This is the distinction between REML and ML for a variance component, and reml() is the default for the same reason: profiling a fixed effect leaves the variance estimate biased downwards.

set.seed(11)
n_out <- 250
d_out <- data.frame(z = sort(runif(n_out, -3, 3)))
d_out$y <- sin(1.4 * d_out$z) + rnorm(n_out, sd = 0.4)

f_reml <- statmod(y ~ s(z, k = 8), gaussian1_distrib(), d_out)
f_ml   <- statmod(y ~ s(z, k = 8), gaussian1_distrib(), d_out,
                  outer_criterion = ml())

c(reml = hyper(f_reml)$estimate, ml = hyper(f_ml)$estimate)
#>     reml       ml 
#> 1.262835 1.261774
c(reml = sum(f_reml@edf$edf), ml = sum(f_ml@edf$edf))
#>     reml       ml 
#> 8.043889 8.044375

The unpenalized directions here are the two intercepts and the smooth’s linear column, three of the model’s nine coefficients, the Demmler-Reinsch penalty being \(\operatorname{diag}(0, 1, \ldots, 1)\) and leaving that column alone. ml() profiles them rather than integrating them, so it shrinks slightly less, and the gap widens with the number of such directions.

9.3.2 The exact gradient

The criterion is differentiable in the hyperparameters, and its derivative is available in closed form rather than by differencing. Every piece comes from the implicit function theorem at the penalized mode: differentiating the mode’s own condition \(\partial(-\ell + \rho)/\partial\beta = 0\) in a hyperparameter gives

\[ (H + S)\,\frac{\partial\hat\beta}{\partial\theta_k} \;=\; -\frac{\partial^2\rho}{\partial\beta\,\partial\theta_k} , \]

and the determinant’s derivative follows from the same solve. What the gradient needs of the model is that the information be the observed one and that each penalty’s Hessian be linear in its hyperparameters, which covers s(), te() and any quadratic penalty. Where it exists it is handed to the search and lbfgs() becomes the default optimizer; where it does not, the search compares values.

Measured in evaluations of the criterion, each of them a whole refit of the coefficients, against a derivative-free search: 40 against 32 with one smoothing parameter, 40 against 135 with two, 41 against 269 with three, and 12 against 283 with three and a modeled scale. It does not pay in one dimension and pays from two on, a simplex needing a vertex per dimension where a quasi-Newton method does not.

9.3.3 Prediction error

The other family scores an estimate of how well the fit would predict:

\[ C(\theta) \;=\; -2\,\ell(\hat\beta(\theta)) + \kappa\,\tau(\theta), \qquad \tau(\theta) = \operatorname{tr}\bigl[(H+S)^{-1}H\bigr] , \]

minimized, with \(\kappa\) the price of one degree of freedom: aic() charges 2 and bic() charges \(\log n\). cv() scores the deviance on held-out folds instead, refitting on each.

These are differentiated exactly as well, and out of the same pieces, but one thing separates the two families. The envelope theorem does not apply here: \(\ell\) alone is not stationary at the penalized mode, only the penalized objective is, so \(\mathrm{d}\ell/\mathrm{d}\theta\) carries \(\mathrm{d}\hat\beta/\mathrm{d}\theta\) from the first order. What makes that computable is the mode’s own condition again, \(\partial\ell/\partial\beta = \partial\rho/\partial\beta\).

Generalized cross-validation is not among them. The classical form divides a residual sum of squares by \((n - \tau)^2\), which estimates an unknown scale; here every distribution parameter carries its own equation and nothing is profiled, so there is no unknown scale for that ratio to estimate and the criterion degenerates to aic().

9.3.4 A penalty with a kink is swept, not solved

A marginal criterion expands around the penalized mode. For a lasso that mode sits at the kink for every coefficient the penalty sets to zero, and the second derivative the expansion wants does not exist there. Such a hyperparameter is therefore not estimated by reml() or ml() at all; it is swept along a path of its own values and scored by a prediction-error criterion, bic() by default.

The path is geometric in the size of the kink rather than in the hyperparameter, since the two need not be the same quantity: the size is read from the penalty by probing its subdifferential, which covers an elastic net’s \(\lambda\alpha\) and a Laplace prior written by its scale, whose kink narrows as the hyperparameter grows. It starts at the value that empties the block and descends to a fraction of it, and that top depends on the data and on the rest of the model, being found at the coefficients in hand rather than at a refitted null.

The reason for a path rather than a search is the shape of the problem. The penalized mode is only piecewise smooth in such a hyperparameter, turning a corner whenever a coefficient joins the active set or leaves it, so the criterion inherits the corners and a gradient search would read a slope that is about to change.

set.seed(12)
n_l <- 200
X <- matrix(rnorm(n_l * 12), n_l, 12)
d_lasso <- data.frame(y = X %*% c(1.4, -0.9, 0.7, rep(0, 9)) + rnorm(n_l, sd = 1))
d_lasso$X <- X

f_lasso <- statmod(y ~ lasso(X), gaussian1_distrib(), d_lasso)
hyper(f_lasso)[, c("name", "estimate", "held", "source")]
#>     name estimate  held source
#> 1 lambda 27.91254 FALSE    bic
sum(coef(f_lasso)$mu != 0)
#> [1] 4

Of the twelve columns offered, the chosen point keeps the three that carry signal and the unpenalized intercept beside them.

Where the penalty has a kink, \(\tau\) is restricted to the coordinates away from it, so for a lasso it is the number of surviving coefficients, which is the unbiased count of Zou, Hastie, and Tibshirani (2007). No special case is written for it: the penalty is linear away from the kink, so its second derivative vanishes there and the trace reduces to that number on its own.

9.3.5 What the count leaves out

\(\tau = \operatorname{tr}[(H+S)^{-1}H]\) reads the smoothing parameters as though they were known, and they were not: they were chosen from the same data, so a criterion built on the count is too generous. Propagating their uncertainty into the coefficients gives the corrected variance and count

\[ V' \;=\; V_\beta + J V_\theta J', \qquad J = \frac{\partial\hat\beta}{\partial\theta}, \qquad \tau' = \operatorname{tr}(V' H), \]

with \(J\) the solve above and \(V_\theta\) the inverse of the negated Hessian of the outer criterion, everything on the hyperparameter’s link scale, which is where that criterion optimizes and therefore the only scale on which its Hessian is a variance. This is the first of the two terms Wood, Pya, and Säfken (2016) sum into their corrected count; the second exists there because a Gaussian scale is profiled out of the fit and its uncertainty has to be added back, and here that scale carries an equation and coefficients of its own, so its uncertainty is already inside \(H\).

For a kinked penalty the correction is exactly zero, and that is a refusal rather than an approximation: the map from the hyperparameter to the penalized mode turns a corner whenever the active set changes, and a delta method needs a derivative that is not there.

The same distinction governs what a hyperparameter’s own uncertainty can be. One a marginal criterion maximized is the root of a derivative, so the curvature of that criterion is a variance and summary() prints a standard error and an interval, built on the free scale and mapped back. One chosen along a path is the argument of a minimum over a grid, and no standard error follows from it. hyper()’s source column is what tells the two apart.

9.4 Structural terms in the fit

Most terms of Chapter 8 add columns to a design. A structural term rewrites the likelihood instead, and two shapes of it are fitted here. A filter produces a level of its own, evolving with the data, which is added to the predictor of the equation it sits in. A term of the mixture shape reports no predictor at all: its contribution is a likelihood averaged over latent states, and the model’s log-likelihood comes from the term rather than from the density at any single point.

At most one such term is allowed in a formula, whatever equations they would sit in. Two filters in one equation are two levels adding up, which is one level written twice; in two equations each would be driven by a score that depends on the other’s state, which is one recursion written as two and implemented by neither.

9.4.1 A filter inside the system

The level of Section 9.1.3, \(f_t = \omega + \sum_r a_r s_{t-r} + \sum_j b_j f_{t-j}\), is driven by scores read at the predictors the coefficients also enter. So the derivative of the log-likelihood in a coefficient is not the design column times the score: the level at time \(t\) carries the coefficient through every earlier step of the recursion.

Propagating that forward would cost one derivative array per coefficient. It is taken backwards instead, in one pass, and what the term returns is two things: the derivative of the objective in the static predictor, and the derivative in the score sequence. The second is what keeps the contract small. The term knows its recursion and the model knows its likelihood, so multiplying the second by a mixed second derivative of the log-density gives the derivative in another equation’s predictor without the term ever learning that there are several.

The curvature is a separate matter and was for a while the weaker half. The matrix a scoring step inverts is assembled as though the level were an offset, which is a legitimate matrix to take a step with and is not the information. Writing \(u\) for the coefficients followed by the term’s own parameters, \(V_{q,t}\) for the derivative of equation \(q\)’s predictor in \(u\), and \(E_t\) for the second derivative of the level,

\[ -\frac{\partial^2 \ell}{\partial u\, \partial u'} \;=\; -\sum_t w_t \sum_{q,r} \ell_{qr,t}\, V_{q,t}'\,V_{r,t} \;-\; \sum_t w_t\, \ell_{p,t}\, E_t . \]

Only the equation carrying the filter has a \(V\) that is not simply its own design; there it is the forward Jacobian of the recursion, returned beside the contracted \(E\). The third derivatives the second sum needs are the family’s, in closed form for every one of them, so the exact curvature of a score-driven model rests on the third-order surface of Section 3.2.6. vcov() inverts that joint matrix and returns the coefficient block, which is why a standard error here is not the one a fit with the level frozen would report.

set.seed(21)
n_gas <- 600
om <- 0.3; a1 <- 0.4; b1 <- 0.7
lev <- numeric(n_gas); yg <- numeric(n_gas); lev[1] <- om / (1 - b1)
for (t in seq_len(n_gas)) {
  yg[t] <- rnorm(1, lev[t], 1)
  if (t < n_gas) lev[t + 1] <- om + a1 * (yg[t] - lev[t]) + b1 * lev[t]
}

f_gas <- statmod(y ~ gas(p = 1, q = 1) - 1, gaussian1_distrib(),
                 data.frame(y = yg))
round(f_gas@structural[[1]]$parameter, 4)
#>  omega alpha1  pacf1 
#> 0.3014 0.4137 0.7378

against \(\omega = 0.3\), \(a_1 = 0.4\) and \(b_1 = 0.7\). The persistence is reported here as pacf1 because that is the coordinate the fit runs on: the stationary region of an autoregression is not a box, so the persistence rides a partial autocorrelation chart and no collection of scalar links would keep it inside. At \(q = 1\) the coordinate and the coefficient coincide; above it they do not, and the term reports the coefficients under the literature’s names separately.

The - 1 is not decoration. Written with an intercept the equation would carry a constant and a level that are exactly confounded, and Section 9.1.3 is where that is set out; the fit would hold the term’s \(\omega\) at zero and let the intercept carry it.

9.4.2 A likelihood mixed over states

A latent Markov term gives each observation a level drawn from one of \(K\) regimes, the regime following a chain of its own. What the model maximizes is the mixed likelihood, computed by a normalized forward recursion, and every derivative follows from Fisher’s identity: the derivative of that mixed likelihood in any predictor the model carries is the posterior-weighted derivative of the ordinary one. So the layer differentiates its own log-density once per state, vectorized over the sample, and weights by the smoothed state probabilities. No second recursion is needed for the gradient, and the predictor reported for the equation is the posterior-weighted one, which is what a fitted value means here.

The observed information is not Louis’s identity. That route writes the observed information as the complete-data one minus the variance of the complete-data score, and the second term involves every pair of times, which the smoothed marginals do not give. What is used instead is second-order propagation through the recursion that already computes the likelihood: \(\log L = \sum_t \log c_t\) exactly, so differentiating that arithmetic twice gives the observed Hessian with no identity invoked and no pairwise smoothed probabilities. Louis’s relation becomes the check rather than the method, the missing-information principle requiring the complete-data information to dominate the observed one, with the gap going to zero as the regimes separate and the states become effectively observed.

The distinction is not academic. The complete-data matrix is what a scoring step inverts, and it is positive definite and legitimate for that; used as a variance it understates a standard error by up to 30 per cent, and by most in the middle of the range, where the states are neither well separated nor hopelessly overlapped.

set.seed(22)
n_reg <- 400
state <- numeric(n_reg); state[1] <- 1
for (t in 2:n_reg) {
  state[t] <- if (runif(1) < 0.05) 3 - state[t - 1] else state[t - 1]
}
d_reg <- data.frame(y = rnorm(n_reg, c(-1.5, 1.5)[state], 0.6))

f_reg <- statmod(y ~ regime(k = 2) - 1, gaussian1_distrib(), d_reg)
round(f_reg@structural[[1]]$parameter, 4)
#>  level1    gap2  alr1.1  alr2.1 
#> -1.4878  2.9594  2.8536 -3.1831

The levels are parametrized as a first level and a positive gap, so they come out ordered, and that is a requirement rather than a convenience: \(K\) exchangeable regimes give \(K!\) identical maxima, and an unordered parametrization would let a search wander between them. The chain’s own transition probabilities are carried on the simplex charts of Section 5.2.7, one per row.

9.4.3 Break-points integrated out

A break-point that varies by group can be given a distribution rather than a coefficient, and integrated out. The obvious cost is prohibitive: with \(K\) break-points and \(n\) observations the positions partition the sample in \((n+1)^K\) ways.

The structure removes the exponent in \(n\). The latent positions are independent, and the indicator \(\mathbf{1}(x \ge \psi_k)\) is monotone in the sorted covariate, so the process of which break-points are already passed is itself a hidden Markov chain on the \(2^K\) side patterns, whose transitions factorize over the coordinates. The exact forward recursion then costs \(nK2^K\) rather than \((n+1)^K\), and everything the term reports — the likelihood, the Jacobian, the posteriors and the observed Hessian — propagates through that one recursion. What remains exponential is \(K\) alone, which is bounded at eight and in practice small.

Such a term is of the mixture shape, so it reaches the fit through the same branch regime() does, with the component levels read per observation rather than per state. The prior’s own parameters are estimated by plain maximum likelihood alongside everything else: the prior is part of the likelihood here, not a penalty, so no hyperparameter of it is declared and no criterion of Section 9.3 is put to it.

9.5 Where a fit begins and ends

Two questions bracket the fitting of Section 9.2 and are not part of it: where the iteration starts, and whether the point it stops at is the one the model asks for.

9.5.1 Where it begins

The default is the maximum likelihood estimate of the model with every covariate removed. The model with no covariates is the model with every slope set to zero, so its estimate is exactly where the model with covariates should begin, and it costs one small fit on an intercept-only design. Every other coefficient starts at zero, which for a penalized block is where its penalty is smallest.

Two adjustments make that usable where a naive intercept would not be, and both were found by a fit that did not converge rather than by reasoning. An offset is subtracted before the value is written, since without it a model whose offset averages 6.74 begins at \(e^{0.897 + 6.74} = 2080\) against a sample mean of 2.45 — measured on a count model over person-years, 4.9 seconds and nine scoring iterations with the correction against more than twenty-five minutes without. And the value is written to a parametric intercept and to nothing else: a nonlinear term names the intercept of each of its own parameters (Intercept) too, and those live on that parameter’s own chart rather than on the predictor’s, so a predictor-scale value written onto a parameter riding a log link became \(e^{23.9} = 2.5 \times 10^{10}\).

A term with parameters of its own says where they start, since only the term knows what zero means on its charts: a score-driven term’s loadings begin at a small positive value through their link rather than at zero, which for it is the model without the term.

Where the likelihood is multimodal none of that is enough, and a global search can be run once, before the fit. Three properties of it are worth stating, because each is a decision rather than a detail. It runs once, before the alternation between the coefficients and the hyperparameters begins, and not inside the inner optimizer, where it would rerun at every hyperparameter the outer search tries and return the same answer each time. It runs on the likelihood with the penalties off, because what a starting value has to get right is the basin, and at a probe value representing nobody’s choice a penalty would explore a different model. And it searches only the non-convex coordinates, which the toolkit can name: a structural term’s parameters, the coefficients of a term that recomputes its own block, and each equation’s intercept. A smooth or a random effect is convex and the scoring step reaches its optimum from anywhere; a penalized coordinate is excluded wherever it sits, including inside a non-convex term, since on the likelihood alone nothing identifies a deviation whose penalty is what identifies it.

The hyperparameters cannot be searched from there at all. The objective is the likelihood with the penalties off, in which a hyperparameter does not appear, so no proposal could change one; a global search over them is a search over the criterion of Section 9.3, where each proposal costs a whole refit.

9.5.2 Where it ends

An optimizer’s convergence flag says whether a search stopped on its own rule, which is a statement about the search. What a reader wants is a property of the point, and the two are not the same: measured on one model, the default reported success at a criterion of \(-1783.47\) while the same data under a different optimizer reached \(-1664.43\) and reported failure.

The certificate takes three readings at the reported point, independent of the path taken to it: the outer criterion’s gradient, how far the coefficients sit above their own penalized mode, and which hyperparameters have run to a boundary. It refits nothing.

The state comes from the gradient, and the mode error is reported beside it rather than folded into it. That division is measured rather than chosen. Across shapes the outer gradient separates by five orders of magnitude — 4.7e-07, 7.8e-07, 5.8e-05, 7.7e-05 and 3.0e-04 on fits that are right, against 28.8 on one that is not — while the mode error does not: it reads between 1.8e-16 and 6.1e-12 on four of them, 22.8 on the failing one, and 0.114 on a random-changepoint fit whose answer is right to a correlation of 0.9932. A number that does not separate cannot decide a state, and one that says how far from the mode is worth more than a boolean hiding it.

set.seed(24)
n_ce <- 400
d_ce <- data.frame(x = sort(runif(n_ce, -3, 3)),
                   g = factor(rep(1:20, length.out = n_ce)))
d_ce$y <- rnorm(n_ce, 1 + sin(1.2 * d_ce$x) + rnorm(20, sd = 0.5)[d_ce$g],
                exp(-0.7 + 0.25 * d_ce$x))

f_ce <- statmod(y ~ s(x, k = 8) + random(~ 1 | g) | sigma ~ s(x, k = 5),
                gaussian1_distrib(), d_ce)
ce <- statmod_certificate(f_ce)
c(state = ce$state, gradient = signif(ce$gradient, 3),
  mode_error = signif(ce$mode_error, 3), boundary = ce$boundary)
#>                      state                   gradient                 mode_error 
#>                 "boundary"                    "3e-04"                 "5.59e-10" 
#>                   boundary 
#> "sigma/s(x, k = 5)/lambda"

boundary is a certified state and not a complaint. A hyperparameter may run to an edge and belong there, and here one has: the data were generated with \(\log\sigma\) linear in \(x\), the scale’s smooth is therefore being driven onto its own null space, and its smoothing parameter reaches \(3.2\times 10^{10}\). The criterion is genuinely flat out there, so calling the fit unconverged would be wrong.

A coordinate is reported as sitting at a boundary when its free value exceeds a threshold and its own gradient component has already met the tolerance. Because of that second condition the threshold cannot change the verdict: a coordinate it moves out of the interior had already passed the test, so what the threshold decides is how the point is described and never whether it is certified. The default separates the measured cases with room on both sides, coordinates that ran to an edge sitting at 9.3, 10.5 and 20.6 on the free scale against 0.13, 0.30 and 2.01 for those that did not — and 24.2 for the one above.

Two kinds of model have no outer gradient and are answered differently. One with no penalty at all has no hyperparameter for a gradient to be about, so the only question left is whether the inner fit reached its mode and the mode error answers it. One whose only hyperparameters are kinked gets neither reading and is reported as unknown: at a coefficient the penalty has set to zero the score does not vanish but lies in the subdifferential, so the mode error there is not a statement about being at a mode at all.

9.6 Reading and using a fit

A fitted model holds three things: the specification, kept whole so the model can be read back and reapplied; the coefficients, hyperparameters and structural parameters the fit reached; and the record of how it got there. The accessors below read those, and each of them answers per distribution parameter rather than over one flat vector, because the coefficients of a mean and of a scale are different quantities on different scales.

set.seed(13)
n_rd <- 300

9.6.1 The two variances

Writing \(H\) for the information of the log-likelihood and \(S\) for the second derivative of the penalty,

\[ V_\mathrm{b} = (H+S)^{-1}, \qquad V_\mathrm{f} = (H+S)^{-1} H (H+S)^{-1} . \]

The first is the posterior variance under the prior the penalty is the negative logarithm of, and it is what an interval around a penalized term should be built from: it carries the smoothing bias as though it were variance, which is why such intervals cover at about their nominal rate. The second is the sampling variance of the penalized estimator at a fixed penalty, which is smaller and covers less. With nothing penalized \(S = 0\) and the two coincide.

vcov() gives the first by default, on the same information the fit itself inverted. A coefficient a kinked penalty has set to zero has no row at all: the penalty is not twice differentiable there, so no curvature can be read, and the entry is missing rather than zero. The coefficients such a penalty left alone do get a variance, and it is conditional on that selection.

Every interval is built on the scale that keeps its quantity in its own set and mapped back, exactly as Section 3.3.4 builds one for a single distribution, so a scale’s interval cannot contain a negative number.

9.6.2 Which likelihood

logLik() answers with the conditional log-likelihood: the density at the fitted coefficients, a penalized coefficient among them, paired with the effective degrees of freedom of Section 9.3.5. A criterion built on that pair is the conditional AIC, and it asks how well the model describes the groups and curves actually observed.

A mixed-model package reports the marginal likelihood instead, with the random effects integrated out and the count the number of estimated parameters. That asks about the population, and its AIC is a different number. Neither convention allows the halves to be mixed, a marginal likelihood against an effective count or a conditional one against a parameter count (Vaida and Blanchard 2005).

c(conditional = as.numeric(logLik(f_rd)),
  marginal    = as.numeric(logLik(f_rd, type = "marginal")))
#> conditional    marginal 
#>   -223.9782   -279.3186
c(edf = attr(logLik(f_rd), "df"),
  npar = attr(logLik(f_rd, type = "marginal"), "df"))
#>      edf     npar 
#> 22.40404  5.00000

The marginal value is the one the outer criterion maximized, so it exists only where a marginal criterion actually ran. Where the hyperparameters were held or chosen along a path there is none, and asking for one signals an error rather than returning a number that would look like one.

Measured on a random intercept over 120 groups, the two readings of one fit are \(-4148.59\) on 115.72 effective degrees of freedom and \(-4372.79\) on 4, against lme4’s marginal \(-4371.71\) on 4: the marginal halves agree and the conditional pair is a different quantity.

9.6.3 Prediction

predict() answers per parameter, on the parameter scale or the predictor’s, and will give a moment of the family instead if asked for one. Each term’s blueprint is reapplied rather than rebuilt, so the knots of a smooth and the levels of a factor are those the model was fitted with. The identity that pins it is that predicting on rows the model saw returns the fitted values there.

nd <- data.frame(x = c(-1, 0, 1),
                 g = factor(c(1, 2, 3), levels = levels(d_rd$g)))
predict(f_rd, "mu", newdata = nd, se = TRUE)
#>         fit        se     lower     upper
#> 1 -1.480255 0.1104639 -1.696760 -1.263749
#> 2  1.838281 0.1140318  1.614783  2.061779
#> 3  1.472762 0.1210629  1.235483  1.710041

A standard error is the delta method over the variance above, so it inherits which of the two was asked for. It is NA for an observation whose predictor reads a coefficient that has no variance, which is the truth about such a fit rather than a gap in the arithmetic.

A term carrying state is continued past the end of its series by handing predict() time indices that were not observed. The continuation is deterministic: a score has zero conditional mean, so past the data the driving term sits at its expectation and the recursion runs on with the loadings contributing only while they still reach an observed time. No standard error is reported there, because the uncertainty of a forecast is mostly the uncertainty of the future scores and not of the parameters, and reporting the second under the name of the first would understate it.

9.6.4 Simulating from a model

rstatmod() draws from a model at coefficients the caller supplies, which is what a simulation study needs. The truth comes back beside the data rather than attached to it: a list holding the data, the coefficients, the per-observation parameters and whatever a term with state drew.

sim <- rstatmod(y ~ x | sigma ~ x, gaussian1_distrib(),
                data.frame(x = runif(200, -2, 2)),
                par = list(mu = c(1, 0.8), sigma = c(-0.7, 0.2)))
names(sim)
#> [1] "data"       "par"        "theta"      "latent"     "structural" "n_sim"     
#> [7] "call"
round(coef(statmod(y ~ x | sigma ~ x, gaussian1_distrib(), sim$data))$mu, 3)
#> (Intercept)           x 
#>       1.022       0.811

The predictor is assembled as a fit assembles it, so a term whose block moves with its coefficients contributes what the term computes and not its block times them; the two differ by the whole nonlinearity.

Two choices in it decide what a study measures. Passing data holds the covariates fixed, so what is measured is the estimator’s behavior conditional on that design; passing covariates, one function per column, draws them afresh at every replicate and measures over the design as well. Neither is more correct and a study should say which it ran. And with several replicates the truth is drawn once and shared, since a study over replicates measures the variability of an estimator at a set of parameters, so the replicates differ in what is random rather than in what is being estimated.

9.7 In practice

Two models fitted and read from end to end. The first is the one the framework exists for, an equation on every parameter of the response; the second carries a term whose design block moves with its own coefficients.
