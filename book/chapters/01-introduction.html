1  Introduction

1.1 Duplicated infrastructure

Almost every R modelling package contains, somewhere in its internals, its own private copy of the Gamma distribution, written as a handful of helper functions. Typically there is a switch on a character string that returns a density, a closure that computes a score, and a block inside a fitting routine that produces the one second derivative that routine needs. The same is true of link functions. Every package that fits something positive carries its own log link, and every package that fits a probability carries its own logit.

None of these objects can be reused, because none of them is really an object. They are code paths, private to the package that owns them, and they contain only what their author’s routine happened to call for — usually the density and the score, sometimes the Hessian, almost never anything beyond. A package that needs a third derivative writes its own Gamma from scratch; a package that wants derivatives with respect to an unconstrained parameter does the same. The result is that the R ecosystem contains dozens of Gamma distributions, each partial and each capable of being wrong in its own way.

The observation the toolkit is built on is that all of this is unnecessary, because a Gamma distribution is a fixed mathematical object. It does not vary from package to package. Its density, score, information, third and fourth derivatives, derivatives with respect to the response and behaviour under reparametrisation are all determined by the definition, once and for all. They can therefore be written down once, correctly and completely, for everyone to build on.

That is what statmodels7 is: infrastructure meant to be reused, rather than another modelling package. The measure of its success is not how well it fits models but whether somebody else’s modelling package can be built on top of it.

1.2 The contents of a complete implementation

The principle applies to both kinds of object the toolkit currently provides, and it takes a different form for each, because what counts as complete differs.

A link function is a map between a constrained parameter and an unconstrained one, and what a modelling routine can ask of it is its value and its derivatives — in both directions. Almost every implementation in R carries the first derivative of the inverse link and stops there, which is exactly what a GLM fitted by iterative least squares needs and nothing more. A link in this toolkit carries four derivatives of the forward map and four of the inverse, so it can serve a routine working at any order up to the fourth. It also knows its own domain, so the routine does not have to.

A distribution supplies, or can derive, the following. It has the density or mass function, the cdf, the quantile function and a random generator. It has the score, the observed information and the expected information, plus third and fourth derivatives of the log-likelihood, both observed and expected. It has derivatives with respect to the response as well as the parameters, and derivatives of the distribution function, which is what a censored observation contributes. Finally, it provides every one of these with respect to the unconstrained parameters as well as the natural ones.

The items at the end of each list are the ones usually missing elsewhere, and they are precisely the ones a modelling framework depends on. Derivatives in the response are what quantile-type residuals and smoothness penalties are made of. Derivatives on the unconstrained scale are what an optimiser wants: a Gamma variance must stay positive, and an algorithm forced to respect that constraint is doing constrained optimisation, whereas an algorithm working with \(\eta = \log \sigma^2\) moves freely over the whole real line. Third and fourth derivatives, finally, are the raw material of higher-order asymptotics and bias correction.

This is also where the two packages meet, and why neither would be much use alone. Transporting a fourth derivative of a log-likelihood from the natural scale to the unconstrained one is an application of Faà di Bruno’s formula, and it consumes exactly the four derivatives of the inverse link that linkfunctions7 carries — Section 3.1.4 works this out in detail. A link stopping at second order would silently cap the whole toolkit at second-order methods, however much the distributions knew about themselves.

1.3 The toolkit

The name encodes a convention: every package name ends in 7, the 7 being the S7 object system everything is built on.

The toolkit
Package What it provides
numericals7 the numerical layer everything above consumes: jets that differentiate a plain R map to fourth order, one finite-difference stencil library, quadrature and series vectorized over the parameters, and the special functions with their overflow discipline
linkfunctions7 16 link classes with exact analytical derivatives to fourth order, in both directions
distributions7 38 univariate and 4 multivariate distributions with exact score, information and third/fourth derivatives, one name per parametrisation where a family has several, plus wrappers, transformations, closed-form moments and maximum likelihood
optimizers7 11 optimisation algorithms as objects, with composable stopping rules, safeguards that report themselves, starting values generated on the unconstrained scale, and box constraints removed by reparameterisation
basis7 bases as objects: evaluation, derivatives of any order, the integral anchored at the lower endpoint, and exact inner products; B-splines, Fourier and Legendre, products of them for several variables, and the linear transformations that orthonormalise, constrain and diagonalise
parameters7 constrained parameters: a map from an unconstrained vector to a matrix, a simplex or a transition matrix, exact to fourth order, with the log-determinant and the solves; rank-deficient matrices admitted, since that is what a penalty is
modelterms7 planned — model terms usable directly in formulas
penalties7 planned — penalties
statmodels7 the meta-package that installs and attaches the members; later also the modelling framework itself

The destination is the last row: a framework in the spirit of the generalized additive models for location, scale and shape of Rigby and Stasinopoulos (2005), described at length in Stasinopoulos et al. (2017). In that framework every parameter of a distribution can be modelled rather than just the mean, the optimiser is pluggable, and estimation can be frequentist or Bayesian. Users can also define new model terms and drop them straight into formulas. Everything below it exists to make that possible, and one design pressure has shaped it all: exact derivatives, on the unconstrained scale, for components the framework has never seen before.

1.4 Extending the toolkit

A toolkit that worked only for the sixteen links and forty-two distributions it ships with would have solved nothing; it would simply be one more closed catalogue, larger than the others. What matters is what happens when a user brings an object the authors never considered. In both packages the object is treated exactly like the ones that came with them: there is no registration step and no separate interface for the “advanced” case.

For a link the requirement is only that the ten generics be implemented, and since a link is a formula and its derivatives, that is a dozen lines. Nothing distinguishes the result from a built-in one: it can be handed to any distribution as the link for any parameter, and the fourth-order machinery will use it without knowing where it came from.

NegLog <- S7::new_class("NegLog", parent = link, package = NULL)

S7::method(linkfun,   NegLog) <- function(x, theta) -log(theta)
S7::method(linkinv,   NegLog) <- function(x, eta)   exp(-eta)
S7::method(dlinkfun,  NegLog) <- function(x, theta) -1 / theta
S7::method(d2linkfun, NegLog) <- function(x, theta)  1 / theta^2
S7::method(dlinkinv,  NegLog) <- function(x, eta)   -exp(-eta)
S7::method(d2linkinv, NegLog) <- function(x, eta)    exp(-eta)

neglog <- NegLog(link_name = "neglog", link_bounds = c(0, Inf), link_params = NULL)
linkinv(neglog, linkfun(neglog, c(0.5, 2)))
#> [1] 0.5 2.0

For a distribution the contract is even smaller, and it is the one that decides whether the package is infrastructure or a catalogue: a new distribution needs nothing but its density. Everything else has a fallback — the cdf is computed by quadrature, the quantile function by root-finding, the random generator by a ratio-of-uniforms scheme that needs only the density, and every derivative by finite differences. Whatever the user later supplies in closed form simply replaces the corresponding fallback, with no registration step and no separate interface for the “advanced” case.

To see what this means in practice, here is a complete definition of the Rayleigh distribution, which has density \(f(y;\sigma) = (y/\sigma^2)\, e^{-y^2/2\sigma^2}\) on \((0, \infty)\):

Rayleigh <- S7::new_class("Rayleigh", parent = continuous_distrib, package = NULL)

S7::method(distrib_pdf, Rayleigh) <- function(distrib, y, theta, log = FALSE) {
  s <- theta[[1]]
  ld <- log(y) - 2 * log(s) - y^2 / (2 * s^2)
  if (log) ld else exp(ld)
}

rayleigh <- Rayleigh(
  distrib_name = "rayleigh", dimension = "univariate", bounds = c(0, Inf),
  params = "sigma", params_interpretation = c(sigma = "scale"), n_params = 1,
  params_bounds = list(sigma = c(0, Inf)),
  link_params = list(sigma = log_link())
)

Nothing above mentions a cdf, a quantile function, a generator or a derivative, and yet all of them are immediately available. The median of a Rayleigh is \(\sigma\sqrt{2\log 2}\), its score is \(y^2/\sigma^3 - 2/\sigma\), and its expected information is \(4/\sigma^2\); the fallbacks recover each of these from the density alone:

theta <- list(sigma = 2)

distrib_quantile(rayleigh, 0.5, theta)     # by root-finding on the numerical cdf
#> [1] 2.35482
2 * sqrt(2 * log(2))                       # the closed form
#> [1] 2.35482

distrib_gradient(rayleigh, c(1, 3), theta) # by finite differences
#> $sigma
#> [1] -0.875  0.125
c(1, 3)^2 / 2^3 - 2 / 2                    # the closed form
#> [1] -0.875  0.125

distrib_expected_hessian(rayleigh, 1, theta)$sigma_sigma   # -4/sigma^2
#> [1] -1

The twenty lines above are a first-class citizen of the toolkit: the object can be zero-inflated, truncated, transformed, and fitted by Fisher scoring exactly like the distributions the package ships with.

1.5 Validators

Because users are expected to write their own distributions and links, the packages ship the tools to validate them. check_link() verifies that a link is invertible in both directions, strictly monotone and consistent with the inverse function theorem, and that each analytical derivative agrees with a numerical one. check_distrib() runs the analogous battery on a distribution. It checks that the density integrates to one, that the cdf and the quantile function invert each other and agree with the density, and that the random generator matches the cdf. It also checks every derivative against a finite-difference computation, and the expected information against a Monte Carlo estimate of the variance of the score.

Both run on any object, including one just defined — here, on the two objects above:

invisible(check_link(neglog))
#> Checking S7 Link Object: neglog 
#>   [1] Invertibility (Theta space): [PASSED] 
#>   [2] Invertibility (Eta space):   [PASSED] 
#>   [3] Strict Monotonicity:         [PASSED] 
#>   [4] Inverse Function Theorem:    [PASSED] 
#>   [5] Link Derivatives:            [PASSED to order 2, 2 numerical] 
#>   [6] Inverse Link Derivatives:    [PASSED to order 2, 2 numerical]
res <- check_distrib(rayleigh, theta = list(sigma = 2), orders = 1:2,
                     nsim = 5e4, verbose = FALSE)
res[, c("check", "status")]
#>                                         check status
#> 1                     density integrates to 1     OK
#> 2                     density is non-negative     OK
#> 3             cdf in [0,1] and non-decreasing     OK
#> 4                 cdf agrees with the density     OK
#> 5                     quantile/cdf round-trip     OK
#> 6                         rng matches the cdf     OK
#> 7              gradient vs finite differences     OK
#> 8               hessian vs finite differences     OK
#> 9         expected information vs Monte Carlo     OK
#> 10 response derivatives vs finite differences     OK
#> 11  link-scale gradient vs finite differences     OK

The link above declares only two derivative orders of the four, and the validator says so rather than passing what it was not given.

basis7 ships check_basis(), which does the same for a basis and reports a quantity it cannot check independently as unchecked rather than as passed.

The validators are used systematically on everything the packages contain. The six chapters that follow present the mathematics behind all of it, one per package: Chapter 2 for linkfunctions7, Chapter 3 for distributions7, Chapter 4 for optimizers7, Chapter 5 for basis7, Chapter 6 for parameters7, and Chapter 7 for numericals7.
