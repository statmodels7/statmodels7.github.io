8  The numericals7 package

Every chapter so far has leaned on a small amount of numerical machinery: finite-difference stencils behind the fallback derivatives of Chapter 2 and Chapter 3, quadrature behind the expectations of Section 3.2, combinatorial enumerations behind the chain rules of Section 3.1.4. numericals7 holds that machinery once, at the bottom of the toolkit, so that no package above it carries a copy of its own.

Section 8.1 presents the single stencil library – the weights of a finite-difference rule for any derivative order at any offsets, from one linear system. Section 8.2 presents quadrature and series summation vectorized over the parameters, which is the shape a regression model needs: the same integrand at hundreds of parameter values, evaluated as a matrix rather than in a loop. Section 8.3 presents the special functions the distributions are written in, each evaluated so that it remains finite over the argument ranges statistical work reaches.

8.1 Finite-difference stencils

A finite-difference rule approximates the \(m\)-th derivative of \(f\) at \(x\) by a weighted combination of values on a grid of offsets, \[ f^{(m)}(x) \;\approx\; \frac{1}{h^m} \sum_{j=1}^{n} w_j\, f(x + o_j h), \] and the weights are determined by exactness on polynomials: substituting \(f(t) = (t - x)^p\) for \(p = 0, \dots, n-1\) gives the linear system \[ \sum_{j=1}^{n} w_j\, o_j^{\,p} \;=\; p!\,[p = m], \qquad p = 0, \dots, n - 1, \] whose matrix is a Vandermonde in the offsets. fd_weights(offsets, order) solves exactly this system, for any derivative order at any distinct offsets, so central, one-sided and staggered rules are all one construction; Fornberg (1988) gives the recursive solution and the classical background.

# the five-point first-derivative weights, times 12: (1, -8, 0, 8, -1)
fd_weights(-2:2, 1L) * 12
#> [1]  1 -8  0  8 -1

# a third derivative of exp at 1, against the function itself
fd_derivative(exp, 1, order = 3L, accuracy = 4L) - exp(1)
#> [1] 3.076484e-09

fd_offsets(order, accuracy) chooses the grid: a central stencil of \(2r + 1\) points with reach \(r = \max\{1, \lceil (m + a)/2 \rceil - 1\}\) evaluates a derivative of order \(m\) to accuracy \(O(h^a)\), and the one-sided variants shift the same number of points to one side of \(x\) for use near a boundary. fd_step(x, order, accuracy) supplies the step, \(\varepsilon^{1/(m+a)} \max(1, |x|)\) with \(\varepsilon\) the machine precision, which balances the \(O(h^a)\) truncation error against the \(O(\varepsilon h^{-m})\) amplification of rounding (Press et al. 2007); when bounds are supplied the step is clamped so the whole stencil stays inside the domain.

What stays out of this package is the policy on how stencils may be used. The rule of Chapter 2 and Chapter 3 – one stencil of order \(k - m\) applied to the highest analytically available order \(m\), never a chain of first differences – belongs to the packages that enforce it, because it is a statement about their derivative contracts and not about the weights. numericals7 supplies the stencil; the consumer decides what it may legitimately be applied to.

8.2 Quadrature and series over many parameters

The integrals a statistical model needs come in families. An expectation under a fitted distribution is one integral per parameter value, and in a regression model the parameters vary by observation, so a single quantity – a mean, a normalizing constant, an expected information entry – becomes hundreds of integrals of one integrand at as many parameter rows. A scalar integrator called in a loop pays its adaptive machinery once per row. quad_vec() treats the parameter dimension as a matrix dimension instead: the integrand receives a matrix of evaluation points together with a row index saying which parameter set each row belongs to, and one call evaluates every node of every panel of every row.

8.2.1 The Gauss-Kronrod pair

Each panel is estimated by the 15-point Kronrod extension of the 7-point Gauss rule: fifteen nodes carrying two sets of weights, so a single matrix of function values yields both an estimate and, from the difference of the two rules, an error for it. The embedded Gauss rule is exact on polynomials to degree 13 and the Kronrod extension to degree 22; the pair is the workhorse of QUADPACK (Piessens et al. 1983), and the Gauss nodes themselves are the classical construction of Golub and Welsch (1969).

r <- gauss_kronrod15()
c(sum(r$wk), sum(r$wg))          # weights on [-1, 1] sum to its length
#> [1] 2 2
sum(r$wg > 0)                    # the Gauss rule uses seven of the fifteen nodes
#> [1] 7

8.2.2 Batched adaptivity

A row converges when the sum of its panel errors fits the budget \(\max(\mathrm{atol}, \mathrm{rtol}\,\lvert I_i \rvert)\); until then its worst panels are bisected, and the splits from every row join the next single evaluation, so one hard row refines its own panels without serializing the others. Judging the sum matters for integrable endpoint singularities. Near a point where the integrand behaves like \(x^{s-1}\) with \(0 < s < 1\) the error is concentrated in the innermost panel however deep the bisection goes; a rule that gave each panel a share of the budget proportional to its length would demand of that panel an accuracy no depth can reach, while the sum converges as soon as the concentrated error is small enough in absolute terms.

# a beta(0.8, 1.2) density diverges at zero and still normalizes in one call
f <- function(x, i) dbeta(x, c(2, 0.8, 3)[i], c(5, 1.2, 0.7)[i])
quad_vec(f, 0, rep(1, 3))
#> [1] 1 1 1

Two failure rules complete the scheme. A row whose panels at the maximum depth already carry more error than the whole budget can never converge, whatever happens elsewhere, and fails at once; a row that cannot reach the budget otherwise fails when nothing is left to split. Either way the row returns NA with a warning naming it, so the failure is explicit.

Infinite endpoints are brought onto a finite interval by the rational maps \(x = a + t/(1-t)\), \(x = b - t/(1-t)\) and \(x = t/(1-t^2)\), whose Jacobians multiply the integrand; rows of different kinds share one call.

shp <- c(0.5, 1, 2, 5, 50)
g <- function(x, i) x * dgamma(x, shape = shp[i], rate = 1)
range(quad_vec(g, 0, rep(Inf, 5)) - shp)   # the means, against the closed form
#> [1] -1.421085e-13  2.317290e-10

8.2.3 Series

series_vec() sums \(\sum_{k \ge k_0} t(k; \theta_i)\) for every parameter row at once, in blocks of terms evaluated as one matrix, with rows retiring as they converge. A row retires when three conditions hold together: the last block contributed less than the budget, its final term is itself below the budget, and the terms are not growing across the block. The third condition is what keeps a hump-shaped term alive before its mode – a Poisson mass at rate 300 opens with a block over \(k = 0, \dots, 63\) that sums to nearly nothing and ends on a term of about \(10^{-52}\), so the first two conditions alone would retire a series that has not begun.

lam <- c(0.5, 4, 300)
series_vec(function(k, i) dpois(k, lam[i]), n = 3)
#> [1] 1 1 1

8.3 Special functions

The functions of this section serve the distributions of Chapter 3. The formulas are classical; what requires statement is the evaluation strategy attached to each, since a direct implementation returns 0/0, Inf or an exact zero over ranges of arguments that ordinary statistical work reaches.

8.3.1 The Mills ratio

Every derivative of a skew normal log-density is built from the inverse Mills ratio \(R(t) = \phi(t)/\Phi(t)\) and its derivative \(R'(t) = -R(t)\{t + R(t)\}\), the latter following from \(\phi'(t) = -t\,\phi(t)\). Below about \(t = -38\) both the density and the distribution function underflow, so the direct quotient is \(0/0\) while the ratio itself is finite and close to \(-t\); mills_ratio() therefore forms it on the log scale, as \(\exp\{\log\phi(t) - \log\Phi(t)\}\).

m <- mills_ratio(c(-60, -40, 0))
m$r                        # finite in the deep tail, sqrt(2/pi) at zero
#> [1] 60.0166574 40.0249688  0.7978846

8.3.2 Owen’s T function

The skew normal distribution function is \(F(z) = \Phi(z) - 2\,T(z, \alpha)\) with \[ T(h, a) \;=\; \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_0^{a} \frac{e^{-h^2 (1 + x^2)/2}}{1 + x^2}\, dx, \] Owen’s T function (Owen 1956). The integrand is bounded and smooth over a finite range, which is a better object to integrate than the density over a half line, and owen_t() sends every element of its input into one batched quad_vec() call. Two identities keep the extremes exact rather than quadrature-bound: \(T(h, -a) = -T(h, a)\), and \(T(h, \infty) = \tfrac{1}{2}\Phi(-\lvert h \rvert)\).

c(owen_t(0, 1) * 2 * pi, atan(1))            # T(0, a) = arctan(a) / (2 pi)
#> [1] 0.7853982 0.7853982
c(owen_t(1.3, Inf), 0.5 * pnorm(-1.3))
#> [1] 0.04840024 0.04840024

8.3.3 The Bessel ratio and its inverse

The von Mises distribution of Chapter 3 is written in \(A(\kappa) = I_1(\kappa)/I_0(\kappa)\), a strictly increasing bijection from \((0, \infty)\) onto \((0, 1)\) – for that family, the mean resultant length. The unscaled Bessel functions overflow past about \(\kappa = 700\), so bessel_i_ratio() takes both exponentially scaled, the shared factor \(e^{\kappa}\) canceling in the ratio. The scaled functions themselves underflow to an exact zero between \(10^5\) and \(10^6\), and past \(\kappa = 10^4\) the ratio is taken from its asymptotic expansion \[ A(\kappa) \;=\; 1 - \frac{1}{2\kappa} - \frac{1}{8\kappa^2} - \frac{1}{8\kappa^3} + O(\kappa^{-4}), \] whose next term is already below the resolution of a double at the switch point (Abramowitz and Stegun 1964), so the ratio is finite and accurate at any argument.

The derivatives follow from the Bessel recurrences \(I_0' = I_1\) and \(I_1' = I_0 - I_1/\kappa\), which combine into \(A' = 1 - A/\kappa - A^2\); differentiating that identity repeatedly writes each order in the orders below it, so the whole table to \(A''''\) costs the two Bessel evaluations of \(A\) and nothing more (bessel_i_ratio_derivs()). \(A'\) is the variance of a cosine and therefore positive, which is what keeps the inverse well defined: bessel_i_ratio_inverse() finds \(\kappa = A^{-1}(\rho)\) by root finding and differentiates it by the inverse function rule, \[ \kappa' = \frac{1}{A'}, \qquad \kappa'' = -\frac{A''}{(A')^3}, \qquad \kappa''' = \frac{3(A'')^2 - A'A'''}{(A')^5}, \] with the fourth order in the same pattern. These derivatives are what let the von Mises in its mean resultant length carry exact link-scale derivatives in Chapter 3.

c(bessel_i_ratio(1000),                       # past the unscaled overflow
  bessel_i_ratio(1e8))                        # past the scaled underflow
#> [1] 0.9994999 1.0000000
inv <- bessel_i_ratio_inverse(0.7)
c(kappa = inv$kappa, round_trip = bessel_i_ratio(inv$kappa))
#>      kappa round_trip 
#>   2.013628   0.700000

Fornberg, Bengt. 1988. “Generation of Finite Difference Formulas on Arbitrarily Spaced Grids.” Mathematics of Computation 51 (184): 699–706.
