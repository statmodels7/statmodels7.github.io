7  The penalties7 package

The chapters so far have assembled the ingredients of a regression model: links, distributions, optimizers, bases, constrained parameters. A penalized or Bayesian model adds one more ingredient, a term that shrinks the coefficients, and almost every package that fits such models carries its penalties as special cases inside the fitting code – matrices assembled by the smooth constructors, an elastic net hard-wired into a coordinate descent, SCAD living inside its own solver. None of these is an object, and none can be asked for its normalizing constant, its derivative with respect to its own tuning parameter, or its null space. This chapter presents the package that writes each penalty once, as an object an estimation routine interrogates.

Section 7.1 sets out the definition: a linear map, a scalar function and hyperparameters, with the Bayesian reading – a penalty is the negative logarithm of a prior density – as the organizing one, and explains why the normalizing constant is kept when nearly all software drops it. Section 7.2 presents the four constructions the package ships: the quadratic penalty, the sum of quadratics with a parameter on each, the separable penalty built from a distributions7 density, and the families defined by their derivative. Section 7.3 presents the quantities a marginal-likelihood criterion consumes and the structured prior whose precision is a parameters7 matrix parameter. Section 7.4 assembles a complete penalized fit by hand, with the prior scale estimated jointly with the coefficients.

7.1 The definition

A penalty on a coefficient vector \(\beta \in \mathbb{R}^q\) is a function

\[ \rho(D\beta;\,\theta), \]

built from three parts: an \(m \times q\) matrix \(D\) selecting what is penalized – the coefficients themselves, their successive differences, the jumps of a segmented term – a scalar function \(\rho\) of the resulting \(m\)-vector, and hyperparameters \(\theta\) governing how strongly. Penalized estimation adds \(\rho\) to the negative log-likelihood; Bayesian estimation reads \(\exp(-\rho)\) as a prior density on \(D\beta\). The two readings use the same object, and the package takes the Bayesian one as the organizing definition because it forces a decision the frequentist reading lets one avoid: what to do with the normalizing constant.

penalties7 keeps the constant. A proper penalty evaluates to exactly the negative logarithm of a normalized density, so that, for the ridge penalty with precision \(\lambda\) – the Gaussian prior \(N(0, \lambda^{-1}I_q)\), written by the parameter that measures the shrinkage rather than by its scale, so that a larger value shrinks harder as it does in every other penalty here –

\[ \rho(\beta;\,\lambda) \;=\; \frac{\lambda}{2}\,\beta'\beta \;-\; \frac{q}{2}\log\lambda \;+\; \frac{q}{2}\log 2\pi . \]

Penalized-likelihood software drops the two trailing terms, because at fixed \(\lambda\) they do not move the coefficient estimate. They do, however, determine everything that treats \(\lambda\) as unknown: joint maximization over \((\beta, \lambda)\) without the constant improves without bound as the prior degenerates, \(\lambda\) running to zero with nothing to hold it, and the degrees of freedom \(\nu\) of a heavy-tailed prior appears only in its constant, so dropping it makes \(\nu\) inestimable. Keeping the constant costs one closed expression per penalty and is what allows the hyperparameters to be estimated rather than tuned.

An improper penalty has no constant to keep. A quadratic penalty whose matrix is rank deficient is a Gaussian that is flat along the null space, and the bounded penalties of Section 7.2 make \(\exp(-\rho)\) non-integrable outright. The quadratic case keeps the proper constant of its non-degenerate part – the rank replaces the dimension, and the log pseudo-determinant replaces the log determinant – which is precisely the constant a marginal-likelihood criterion uses; the bounded case returns the bare \(\rho\) and is_proper() says so.

Each hyperparameter travels with a link function from Chapter 2, so that a consumer optimizes \(\theta\) on the unconstrained scale; the derivatives with respect to \(\theta\) are returned on the parameter scale by every method and carried onto the link scale by the chain rule in one place, exactly as distributions7 does for its parameters. Beyond the value, every penalty answers the derivatives an estimation routine asks: the gradient and Hessian in \(\beta\), the gradient and Hessian in \(\theta\), and the mixed block \(\partial^2 \rho / \partial\beta\,\partial\theta_k\), which joint estimation of coefficients and hyperparameters consumes and which the implicit function theorem turns into the gradient of a profiled objective. A penalty also declares its kink set (the points where \(\rho\) is not differentiable in its argument, empty for the smooth penalties and \(\{0\}\) for the sparsity ones), so that a validator or a solver can avoid the points where \(\rho\) is not smooth.

pen <- ridge_penalty(n_coef = 3)
beta <- c(0.8, -0.4, 1.1)

penalty_value(pen, beta, list(lambda = 0.44))
#> [1] 4.430486
penalty_gradient(pen, beta, list(lambda = 0.44))
#> [1]  0.352 -0.176  0.484

# the mixed block: how the beta-gradient moves when lambda does
penalty_cross(pen, beta, list(lambda = 0.44))$lambda
#> [1]  0.8 -0.4  1.1

7.2 The four constructions

Every penalty the package ships is one of four shapes, and the four cover the penalties regression practice actually uses. A fifth object closes the section and is not a penalty at all: a smoother, which replaces the kink two of the four carry, and which the model terms of Chapter 8 use for the same purpose on a break-point.

7.2.1 The quadratic penalty

The quadratic penalty is the Gaussian prior \(\beta \sim N\!\left(0,\,(\lambda\,D'PD)^{-}\right)\) written as its exact negative log-density,

\[ \rho \;=\; \frac{\lambda}{2}\,(D\beta)'P(D\beta) \;-\; \frac{r}{2}\log\lambda \;+\; \frac{r}{2}\log 2\pi \;-\; \frac{1}{2}\log\operatorname{pdet}(P), \tag{7.1}\]

with \(P\) a fixed positive semidefinite matrix – a Gram matrix from Section 6.3, a difference penalty \(D_k'D_k\) in the manner of Eilers and Marx (1996), or anything the caller supplies – and \(r\) its rank. One eigendecomposition at construction fixes \(r\) by the relative rule of Section 5.3 and stores the exact null basis, so that later membership questions never go through a rank recomputed from assembled arithmetic. Everything is then closed form in \(\lambda\): the gradient is \(\lambda D'PD\beta\), the Hessian \(\lambda D'PD\) constant in \(\beta\), the \(\lambda\)-derivative of the value \(\tfrac{1}{2}(D\beta)'P(D\beta) - r/(2\lambda)\), and the mixed block \(D'PD\beta\).

P <- crossprod(diff(diag(6), differences = 2))
pen <- quadratic_penalty(P)

# an improper prior: flat along the linear polynomials
penalty_rank(pen)
#> [1] 4
is_proper(pen)
#> [1] FALSE

One block, repeated

A smooth fitted once per level of a factor gives the same penalty \(P\) on each of \(m\) blocks, so the penalty of the whole is \(I_m \otimes P\). Building that from the assembled matrix decomposes something \(m\) times larger than it needs to be, and every quantity the constructor wants is available from \(P\) alone: the eigenvalues of \(I_m \otimes P\) are \(P\)’s repeated \(m\) times, so the rank is \(mr\), the log pseudo-determinant is \(m\log\operatorname{pdet}P\) and the null space is \(I_m \otimes N\).

quadratic_penalty(P, blocks = m) takes that route, and the large matrix is never formed or decomposed.

P <- crossprod(diff(diag(6), differences = 2))
pb <- quadratic_penalty(P, blocks = 4)
pa <- quadratic_penalty(kronecker(diag(4), P))
b <- seq(-1, 1, length.out = 24)

c(rank_blocks = penalty_rank(pb), rank_assembled = penalty_rank(pa),
  value_gap = abs(penalty_value(pb, b, list(lambda = 2)) -
                  penalty_value(pa, b, list(lambda = 2))))
#>    rank_blocks rank_assembled      value_gap 
#>    1.60000e+01    1.60000e+01    1.24345e-14

The branch keeps its sparse storage on purpose: penalty_hessian() and the other matrix-valued returns come back as Matrix objects rather than base ones, a construction built to avoid the assembled form having no reason to densify at its own boundary. A consumer writing such a Hessian into a block of its own information has to accept both classes, which is the one place the uniform contract of the other branches is relaxed.

blocks does not combine with a map, and the constructor rejects the pair: a map mixes the blocks, so \(D'(I_m \otimes P)D\) is not block diagonal with the structure blocks names.

7.2.2 The sum of quadratics

A single matrix under a single scale forces every direction to be smoothed alike. A tensor-product smooth wants the opposite: one smoothing parameter per margin, so that a surface may be rough in one direction and smooth in another. additive_penalty() carries

\[ S(\lambda) \;=\; \sum_{k} \lambda_k P_k, \]

and everything a marginal criterion reads follows from one eigendecomposition of that sum: \(\partial_k \log\mathrm{pdet}\,S = \operatorname{tr}(S^{+}P_k)\) and \(\partial^2_{kl} \log\mathrm{pdet}\,S = -\operatorname{tr}(S^{+}P_kS^{+}P_l)\).

The rank needs care, and Section 5.3 is the reason. Counting the eigenvalues of \(S(\lambda)\) above a tolerance answers correctly only while the parameters are comparable: once they differ by orders of magnitude the small contributions sink below any fixed tolerance and are read as zeros, so the rank appears to fall as the fit is smoothed. What does not move is the null space, because the null space of a sum of positive semidefinite matrices is the intersection of theirs and does not depend on the parameters at all. The rank is therefore fixed once, at construction, from the components stacked and individually normalized.

P1 <- crossprod(diff(diag(4), differences = 2))
pen <- additive_penalty(list(kronecker(diag(4), P1),
                             kronecker(P1, diag(4))))
penalty_rank(pen)
#> [1] 12

# the count taken from the assembled matrix, as the parameters spread
sapply(c(1, 1e6, 1e12), function(ratio) {
  S <- kronecker(diag(4), P1) + ratio * kronecker(P1, diag(4))
  ev <- eigen(S, symmetric = TRUE, only.values = TRUE)$values
  sum(ev > 1e-10 * max(ev))
})
#> [1] 12 12  8

The stored rank is the first number at every value of the parameters; the counted one is not.

7.2.3 The separable penalty

The third construction applies a univariate log-density from Chapter 3 coordinatewise:

\[ \rho(t;\,\theta) \;=\; -\sum_{j=1}^{m} \log f(t_j;\,\theta), \qquad t = D\beta . \]

The hyperparameters are the distribution’s free parameters, with their bounds and links read off the distribution object, and every derivative is the distribution’s, reassembled through the map: the gradient is \(-D'\ell^{(y)}\), the Hessian \(-D'\,\mathrm{diag}(\ell^{(yy)})\,D\), the \(\theta\) blocks are the summed score and Hessian, and the mixed block is \(-D'\ell^{(y\theta_k)}\) – the response-parameter derivative of Chapter 3, which exists in closed form for every continuous family and is the reason no separable penalty differences anything.

The classical penalties are named instances of this construction. The lasso is the Laplace in location and rate (laplace2_distrib()) held at zero, so its free hyperparameter is the rate \(\lambda\) and the value is \(\lambda\lVert D\beta\rVert_1\) up to the constant, with the kink at the origin declared by the object; the heavy-tailed prior is the Student t held at zero, whose \(\nu\) is estimable exactly because the constant travels with the density.

Ridge is the exception, and it is an exception to the branch rather than to the rule. The Gaussian held at zero is a separable penalty like the others, but that prior written by its PRECISION is exactly Equation 7.1 at the identity matrix – the same value to the last bit – so it is built there, and its hyperparameter is the \(\lambda\) that construction already carries. Two names for one number is what the choice removes: every penalty here is written on the chart whose hyperparameter measures the shrinkage, and a larger value shrinks harder in all of them. The separable Gaussian remains as the twin the quadratic one is held to, where two independent implementations of one object are compared componentwise with no tolerance to choose, and the certification below does exactly that.

The elastic net is the same construction over the family that is the product of those first two, enet_distrib() held at zero. Writing \(a = \lambda\alpha\) and \(c = \lambda(1-\alpha)\), its value is

\[ \rho(D\beta; \lambda, \alpha) = \lambda\left\{\alpha\lVert D\beta\rVert_1 + \frac{1-\alpha}{2}\lVert D\beta\rVert_2^2\right\} + p\log Z(\lambda, \alpha), \qquad Z = \frac{2M(a/\sqrt{c})}{\sqrt{c}}, \tag{7.2}\]

with \(M\) the Mills ratio. That the constant is available in closed form is what makes both hyperparameters estimable: a penalty written as a formula would have the same minimizer at fixed \((\lambda, \alpha)\) and nothing to say about which pair to choose, because a criterion that compares them compares normalized densities. The mixing weight lives in the open unit interval, the two ends being the lasso and ridge of the paragraph above, which remain penalties of their own.

Two things follow from the mixture that do not from either part. The penalized objective is not differentiable, since the \(\ell_1\) term carries its kink through whatever \(\alpha\) multiplies it, so the elastic net is a non-smooth penalty at every admissible \(\alpha\); and its proximal operator is the composition of the two elementary ones,

\[ \operatorname{prox}_{t\rho}(v) = \frac{\mathcal{S}(v,\; t\lambda\alpha)}{1 + t\lambda(1-\alpha)}, \tag{7.3}\]

the soft threshold of the Laplace part followed by the shrinkage of the Gaussian one, which follows from the stationary condition \((\beta - v)/t + a\,\mathrm{sgn}(\beta) + c\beta = 0\) separating coordinatewise.

pen <- elasticnet_penalty(n_coef = 4)
beta <- c(0.5, 1.4, -0.3, 2.0)
th <- list(lambda = 0.8, alpha = 0.6)

# the value, less its constant, is the elastic net of the coefficients
penalty_value(pen, beta, th) - penalty_value(pen, rep(0, 4), th)
#> [1] 3.024
0.8 * (0.6 * sum(abs(beta)) + 0.4 * sum(beta^2) / 2)
#> [1] 3.024

# and the operator is the threshold followed by the shrinkage
penalty_prox(pen, beta, step = 0.5, th)
#> [1]  0.22413793  1.00000000 -0.05172414  1.51724138
# the fused lasso: Laplace increments through a difference map
pen <- lasso_penalty(map = diff(diag(4)))
beta <- c(0.5, 1.4, -0.3, 2.0)
penalty_gradient(pen, beta, list(lambda = 0.8))
#> [1] -0.8  1.6 -1.6  0.8

penalty_kinks(pen, list(lambda = 0.8))
#> [1] 0

The operator as a table

A proximal step is applied once per coordinate per sweep, at a point that moves every time, so a coordinate descent that called back into R for each one would spend its gain on the calls. Every closed-form operator this branch produces is piecewise linear in the coordinate – the soft threshold has two pieces, the elastic net two, MCP three and SCAD four – so it can be handed over as a table instead of as a function.

penalty_prox_spec() returns that table: one row per coefficient, and per row the cuts, the slopes and the intercepts of the pieces. A compiled loop then evaluates the operator by locating a cut and applying an affine map, naming no family at all.

sp <- penalty_prox_spec(lasso_penalty(n_coef = 3), list(lambda = 1.2),
                        step = 0.5)
rbind(cut = sp$cut[1, ], slope = sp$slope[1, ], icept = sp$icept[1, ])
#>       [,1] [,2]
#> cut    0.6  Inf
#> slope  0.0  1.0
#> icept  0.0 -0.6

The first piece is the interval the threshold sets to zero, of half-width \(t\lambda = 0.6\), and the second is the shrinkage \(v - 0.6\) beyond it. The table is read in the magnitude of the coordinate and the sign restored afterwards, every one of these operators being odd, which is also why one row of cuts describes both tails.

The step enters the table rather than the lookup, so a table built at one step does not serve another; in a coordinate descent the step is \(1/v_j\) and does not move while the working weights are held, which is what makes one table serve a whole sweep. The quadratic branch returns NULL here and is not a gap: its proximal operator is a linear solve rather than a piecewise map, and there is nothing to tabulate.

7.2.4 The families defined by their derivative

SCAD (Fan and Li 2001) and MCP (Zhang 2010) are stated in the literature through \(\rho'\), because the derivative carries their design: a constant slope near zero, so that small coefficients are penalized as under the lasso, and a slope that falls to zero beyond a shoulder, so that large coefficients are left unbiased. On \(t \ge 0\), extended evenly,

\[ \rho'_{\mathrm{SCAD}}(t) = \begin{cases} \lambda & t \le \lambda \\[2pt] \dfrac{a\lambda - t}{a - 1} & \lambda < t \le a\lambda \\[2pt] 0 & t > a\lambda, \end{cases} \qquad \rho'_{\mathrm{MCP}}(t) = \left(\lambda - \frac{t}{\gamma}\right)_{+}, \tag{7.4}\]

with \(a > 2\) and \(\gamma > 1\). The value follows by the closed piecewise antiderivative anchored at \(\rho(0) = 0\), and the \(\theta\) and mixed blocks are closed piecewise forms as well. Both penalties are bounded, so \(\exp(-\rho)\) does not integrate and no constant exists: they are improper by construction, and the value is the bare \(\rho\). The kink set contains the origin, and the second derivative additionally jumps at the region boundaries, which the object reports so that a numerical comparison never straddles them.

pen <- scad_penalty(n_coef = 1)
th <- list(lambda = 1, a = 3.7)

# flat beyond the shoulder: no bias on large coefficients
penalty_value(pen, 5, th) == penalty_value(pen, 50, th)
#> [1] TRUE

7.2.5 Smoothing an absolute value

A kink is what makes the last two constructions non-smooth, and what makes a break-point term’s contribution non-differentiable in its position. Both can be removed the same way, by replacing \(\lvert u\rvert\) with a smooth surrogate. An abs_smoother() is that surrogate as an object: a function \(s(u)\) standing in for the absolute value, carrying its own derivatives to fifth order and a width that says over what scale the replacement acts.

The derivatives are carried as functions rather than as expressions, and that is a decision the quintic forces: a piecewise smoother has branches stats::deriv() cannot read, and writing the derivatives out in the constructor makes those branches ordinary code. Only \(s\) is contracted for; the sign, the step and the hinge follow by composition, \(\operatorname{sign}(u) \approx s'(u)\), \(\mathbb{1}(u>0) \approx (1+s'(u))/2\) and \((u)_+ \approx (u + s(u))/2\).

sm <- smooth_probit()
u <- c(-1, -0.3, 0, 0.3, 1)
rbind(s = smoother_deriv(sm, u, width = 0.3), abs = abs(u))
#>         [,1]      [,2]      [,3]      [,4]     [,5]
#> s   1.000067 0.3499893 0.2393654 0.3499893 1.000067
#> abs 1.000000 0.3000000 0.0000000 0.3000000 1.000000

Three widths out the surrogate and the absolute value agree to four decimals; at the corner \(s(0) = 2h\varphi(0)\), which is the excess the smoothing buys and the scale on which the bias lives.

Three are shipped. smooth_probit() has Gaussian tails, smooth_hyperbolic() is \(\sqrt{u^2+c}\), and smooth_quintic() is exact outside \([-h,h]\) and \(C^3\) within it. The probit earns something the others do not: smoothing with width \(h\) is exactly convolving with \(N(0, h^2)\), so a random quantity smoothed this way has its scale inflated in a known way and the object declares the correction \(\tau^2_{\text{true}} = \tau^2 - h^2\). That is a property of the mollifier and of no model, which is why it belongs on the smoother; the other two declare none and a consumer then reports the apparent scale alone.

The width cannot be arbitrarily small, and the floor is derived rather than chosen. A Jacobian column carries \(s''(0)/2 \sim 1/h\) against columns of the order of the covariate’s range \(D\), so holding the design’s condition number below \(\epsilon^{-1/2}\) requires \(h \gtrsim \sqrt{\epsilon}\,D\). A consumer that resolves a width from the data checks it against that floor.

check_abs_smoother() validates one the way check_link() validates a link, comparing each analytic order against one numerical differentiation of the order below. It earned itself immediately: the quintic’s own third derivative was written with a denominator of 4 where the algebra gives 2, and the check reported a relative error of 0.5 at order three before anything shipped.

7.3 The marginal pieces and the structured prior

A smoothing parameter is not well estimated by maximizing jointly over coefficients and hyperparameters, even with the constant kept; the criterion of choice is the restricted or marginal likelihood, in which the coefficients are integrated out (Wood 2011). For a quadratic penalty that criterion is assembled from three quantities as functions of \(\theta\): the matrix \(\lambda D'PD\) added to the weighted cross-product of the design, its rank, and its log pseudo-determinant

\[ \log\operatorname{pdet}\!\left(\lambda D'PD\right) \;=\; r\log\lambda \;+\; \log\operatorname{pdet}(P), \tag{7.5}\]

linear in \(\log\lambda\) with slope exactly \(r\). The package exposes the three – together with the exact null basis, which is what the model layer intersects across terms when several penalties act on one coefficient block, the rank of a sum being a statement about intersected null spaces rather than about eigenvalues of the assembled matrix (Section 5.3). Only the quadratic penalties answer these questions; for the separable and derivative-defined ones the methods signal an error, because a marginal criterion for a non-Gaussian prior is not a determinant, and a default that computed one anyway would produce silent wrong numbers.

7.3.1 The structured prior

In Equation 7.1 one scale multiplies a constant matrix. The prior of a random effect is richer: its precision has a structure – compound symmetry, an autoregression, a full covariance – whose parameters reach every entry of the matrix. This is the correlated Gaussian prior, and it is the point where penalties7 composes with Chapter 5:

\[ \rho \;=\; \frac{1}{2}\,\beta'\,\Omega(\theta)\,\beta \;-\; \frac{1}{2}\log\operatorname{pdet}\,\Omega(\theta) \;+\; \frac{r}{2}\log 2\pi, \tag{7.6}\]

with \(\Omega\) a parameters7 matrix parameter read as the precision. The hyperparameters are the matrix parameter’s free vector, which is unconstrained by construction, so every link is the identity – the flattening convention of Section 3.5, for the same reason: the constraint is a property of the matrix as a whole, which a scalar link cannot express. Every derivative comes from the parameter’s own contract. Writing \(A_k\) for its first derivative matrices,

\[ \frac{\partial \rho}{\partial \theta_k} \;=\; \frac{1}{2}\,\beta' A_k \beta \;-\; \frac{1}{2}\,\frac{\partial \log\operatorname{pdet}\Omega}{\partial\theta_k}, \qquad \frac{\partial^2 \rho}{\partial\beta\,\partial\theta_k} \;=\; A_k\beta, \tag{7.7}\]

and the second \(\theta\) derivatives add the parameter’s \(A_{kl}\).

Which of the two matrices of the prior the parameter is has to be said, and the parameter says it: its role is read, and a parameter that declares itself usable as either is rejected rather than guessed at, the two readings differing in the sign of the log-determinant term. A parameter declared a covariance is carried onto the precision it implies by the chain rule for an inverse, \(\partial_k \Sigma^{-1} = -\Sigma^{-1} A_k \Sigma^{-1}\), once, so the expressions above are the same arithmetic in both cases. A covariance of deficient rank has no inverse and is rejected; a precision of deficient rank is the improper prior the log pseudo-determinant is written for.

Nothing in the interface changes: the structured penalty answers the marginal pieces through the matrix parameter’s rank, null basis and log-determinant derivatives, so a consumer cannot tell whether the matrix it was handed came from a constant times a scale or from a chart on the positive definite cone.

pen <- structured_penalty(parameters7::ar1(4, role = "precision"))
theta <- list(log_scale = 0.2, z_rho = 0.5)
beta <- c(0.3, -0.1, 0.4, 0.2)

# two hyperparameters reach every entry of the precision
round(penalty_hessian(pen, beta, theta), 3)
#>       v1    v2    v3    v4
#> v1 1.221 0.564 0.261 0.121
#> v2 0.564 1.221 0.564 0.261
#> v3 0.261 0.564 1.221 0.564
#> v4 0.121 0.261 0.564 1.221

unlist(penalty_grad_theta(pen, beta, theta))
#> log_scale     z_rho 
#>  -1.77783   1.52166

At a free vector of zeros the log-Cholesky parameter produces the identity matrix, so the structured prior collapses onto the plain ridge at \(\lambda = 1\) – one more pair of independent constructions of a single object, compared exactly.

7.3.2 What a hyperparameter is about

The hyperparameters of that prior are the free vector of a matrix parameter: logarithms of the diagonal of a Cholesky factor, and the entries below it. Nobody reads those. What the prior is about is the standard deviations and the correlations of the effects it describes, and penalty_readable() is where a penalty says so, in the shape parameters7::param_readable() has for a matrix parameter and distributions7::mv_derived() for a fitted multivariate family.

pen <- distrib_penalty(
  distributions7::fixed(distributions7::mvgaussian_distrib(2),
                        mu1 = 0, mu2 = 0), n_coef = 6)
r <- penalty_readable(pen, list(sigma_log_L1 = 0.2, sigma_log_L2 = -0.1,
                                sigma_L2.1 = 0.5))
rbind(value = round(r$value, 4), scale = r$transform)
#>       sd_v1    sd_v2    cor_v1_v2
#> value "1.2214" "1.0338" "0.4837" 
#> scale "log"    "log"    "atanh"

Two things come with the values. The Jacobian is what a delta-method standard error needs, the reported quantities having variance \(JVJ'\) where \(V\) is the variance of the hyperparameters; and transform says which scale each interval is built on and mapped back from, so that a standard deviation stays positive and a correlation stays inside \((-1, 1)\).

For every other branch the answer is NULL, and that is the right answer rather than a missing method: a smoothing parameter, a rate and a shape are each already the quantity a reader wants, so a consumer reports them as they stand.

7.4 In practice

This section assembles a complete penalized fit from the objects the preceding sections defined, with no fitting function in between: the likelihood comes from the model, the penalty from ridge_penalty(), and the optimization from optimizers7. The example is a Gaussian linear model with a ridge prior on the coefficients, and the point it demonstrates is Section 7.1’s: with the normalizing constant kept, the prior precision \(\lambda\) is estimated jointly with the coefficients by ordinary maximization, with no cross-validation and no separate criterion.

The data are simulated with three active coefficients among eight, and the error standard deviation is treated as known:

set.seed(42)
n <- 120; p <- 8
X <- matrix(rnorm(n * p), n, p)
beta_true <- c(2, -1.5, 1, rep(0, p - 3))
sd_e <- 1.2
y <- drop(X %*% beta_true + rnorm(n, sd = sd_e))

The negative log posterior is the negative log-likelihood plus the penalty, a function of the \(p\) coefficients and of the prior precision. The precision is optimized on its link scale, \(\eta = \log\lambda\), and penalty_grad_theta(scale = "link") returns the matching derivative, so the gradient of the whole objective is assembled from quantities the objects already provide:

library(penalties7)
library(optimizers7)
pen <- ridge_penalty(n_coef = p)

neg_post <- function(par) {
  b <- par[1:p]; th <- list(lambda = exp(par[p + 1]))
  -sum(dnorm(y, drop(X %*% b), sd_e, log = TRUE)) + penalty_value(pen, b, th)
}
neg_post_gr <- function(par) {
  b <- par[1:p]; th <- list(lambda = exp(par[p + 1]))
  c(-crossprod(X, y - drop(X %*% b)) / sd_e^2 + penalty_gradient(pen, b, th),
    penalty_grad_theta(pen, b, th, scale = "link")$lambda)
}

fit <- minimize(lbfgs(), neg_post, par = rep(0, p + 1), gr = neg_post_gr)
fit@converged
#> [1] TRUE

The maximizer shrinks the coefficients towards zero relative to least squares, and the estimated prior precision lands near the reciprocal of the mean square of the coefficients that generated the data, which is the quantity it estimates:

b_map <- fit@par[1:p]
lambda_hat <- exp(fit@par[p + 1])
b_ols <- drop(solve(crossprod(X), crossprod(X, y)))

round(rbind(true = beta_true, ols = b_ols, ridge = b_map), 3)

At a fixed prior precision the maximizing coefficients solve the ridge normal equations with a shrinkage of \(\mathrm{sd}_e^2\lambda\), so the joint maximum must agree with the closed form evaluated at the estimated precision:

lam <- sd_e^2 * lambda_hat
b_closed <- drop(solve(crossprod(X) + lam * diag(p), crossprod(X, y)))
max(abs(b_map - b_closed))
#> [1] 5.058485e-08

Dropping the constant from penalty_value() would remove the \(-\tfrac{q}{2}\log\lambda\) term of Section 7.1, the objective would improve without bound as \(\lambda\) shrinks, and the joint maximization above would drive the penalty to zero. The constant is what makes this section’s one-line estimation possible.

A sparsity penalty replaces the smooth objective with one that has a kink at zero in every coordinate, which penalty_kinks() reports; a fit through lasso_penalty() therefore uses the bundle method of Section 4.3 in place of a gradient method. The marginal-likelihood route for a smoothing parameter, which integrates the coefficients out instead of maximizing over them, consumes the pieces of Section 7.3 and belongs to the model layer.
