Preface

This book is the mathematical companion to the statmodels7 toolkit. Its purpose is to collect in one place the mathematics that the packages implement. That covers the link functions with their derivatives to fourth order, the probability distributions with their scores and information matrices, the operations that build new distributions out of existing ones, and the optimisation methods that fit them.

It is written to be taught from as much as to be consulted, and that has settled two questions of style. The first is what to show. Every formula the toolkit actually evaluates appears on the page, in the form the code uses, together with enough of the reasoning to make it intelligible: where a quantity comes from, what it means, and which condition it depends on. What does not appear is the formal proof. A reader who wants the argument in full is pointed to the paper or the textbook where it is given properly, and the appendix collects those references. The space this saves is spent on explaining the results and the places where the implementation had to make a choice. The second question is how to say it. The mathematics here is elementary throughout — calculus, a little probability, the occasional exchange of derivative and integral — and the prose aims to be continuous and unhurried rather than clipped, so that the chapters can be read straight through.

The mathematics is not new, and no attempt is made to pretend otherwise. What is particular to this book is that the formulas shown are the formulas the packages run, which is a claim about software rather than about mathematics, and whose value rests on the care taken to keep it true.

The book is generated with Quarto directly from the source code of the packages, which are loaded from the working tree each time it is rendered. The numerical examples scattered through the chapters are therefore executed by the very code they illustrate, and the output shown is the output produced at build time.

How the book is organised

After the introduction there is one chapter per package, so that the book grows the way the toolkit does.

1  Introduction explains what the toolkit is for and why it is built the way it is. It contains no mathematics and can be read on its own.

2  The linkfunctions7 package is linkfunctions7: what a link is required to satisfy, how the derivatives of a link and of its inverse determine one another, and why they are carried to fourth order. It ends with a catalogue of the sixteen link classes, each with its formulas written out in full.

3  The distributions7 package is distributions7, and divides into four. Distributions develops the likelihood theory the toolkit rests on — score, observed and expected information, the Bartlett identities, derivatives on the unconstrained scale via Faà di Bruno’s formula, derivatives of the distribution function — and presents the fourteen families. Particular care is taken where the parametrisation differs from the one used by base R. Transformations of distributions treats the operations that turn one distribution into another, and is the most demanding part of the book, since the wrappers compose with any parent and their formulas are therefore stated once, in general, at every order the toolkit supports. Fitting a distribution fits a distribution to a sample by maximum likelihood, which is the smallest problem that exercises all of the preceding machinery at once. Numerical fallbacks describes what a distribution receives when it implements nothing but its density.

4  The optimizers7 package is optimizers7, and divides into four as well. Descent directions and line searches defines descent directions and the conditions a line search must guarantee. Second-order and quasi-Newton methods takes the methods in order of how much curvature they store, from the exact Hessian down to a single number. Non-smooth objectives deals with objectives that have a kink, where the gradient does not become small at the solution and a gradient-based method reaches the answer and reports failure. Box constraints and stochastic gradients covers box constraints, removed by a change of variable, and Adam, whose steps are not descent steps. Finally Starting values and multiple starts covers the choice of the starting point, which reuses the same change of variable, and the question of how many runs to make.

Appendix A — Notation collects the notation used throughout, and the references follow it.
